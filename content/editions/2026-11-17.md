# Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Tuesday, November 17, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. A development bank makes its first Islamic deal

The Black Sea Trade and Development Bank has agreed to lend €25 million (about $27.5 million) to Türkiye Emlak Katılım Bankası for green projects in Türkiye. The five-year deal uses murabaha, a cost-plus sale, and it is the first Islamic finance transaction the development bank has done since it was set up. The agreement was signed in Antalya during COP31, the United Nations climate conference. The first money from the deal is expected to support Türkiye's Deposit Return System for beverage packaging, alongside renewable energy, resource efficiency and sustainable infrastructure projects.

**What to learn:** A development bank is a bank owned by governments that lends to projects commercial banks will not fund on their own. The Black Sea Trade and Development Bank is owned by 11 countries around the Black Sea, including Türkiye and Greece, and it is based in Thessaloniki in Greece. Until now it had never done a deal in Islamic form. This €25 million facility is its first.

The deal uses murabaha, the cost-plus sale covered in the [Oct 8 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-08.html). In a murabaha, the financier buys an asset and sells it to the buyer at a stated markup, and the buyer pays that price over time. Here the facility runs for five years, and Emlak Katılım uses the money to fund green projects. The bank's return comes from the sale, and no interest is charged at any point.

The deal matters because it opens a new source of money for Islamic banks. A development bank brings long-term funding and a stamp of approval that other lenders watch. Emlak Katılım can now pass that funding on to green projects in Türkiye, and other development banks may follow the same path. The facility is the second of its kind from a development bank to a Turkish participation bank.

The people this touches start with an ordinary person in Türkiye returning an empty bottle. Under the Deposit Return System, a shopper pays a small deposit on a bottled drink and gets it back by returning the bottle to a collection point. The first money from this deal helps pay for the machines and collection points that make that system run. The rest goes to renewable energy, recycling, and water and energy saving on farms.

Emlak Katılım already works in this area. The bank says it has prevented 142,240 tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions through more than 2 billion lira (about $40 million at about 50 lira to the dollar) of thematic sukuk issued so far. The development bank's authorised capital is €3.45 billion, so €25 million is a small first step. What matters is that a lender that has never worked in Islamic form now has, and the sale contract is what made it possible.

For a reader in the USA, the parallel is a development bank bond. The World Bank raises money from investors and lends it on for development projects. The difference here is the contract: a sale at a markup instead of an interest-bearing loan. A USA reader cannot buy into this deal. The lesson is the mechanism: a lender that has never worked in Islamic form can do so with a sale contract, and the same shape works for any asset a buyer needs.

## Learn

### Green sukuk: a sukuk that can only fund green projects

A green sukuk is a sukuk whose money can only go to projects that help the environment. The projects are named in advance: solar power, clean transport, waste recycling, water saving, or farming that protects the soil. The sukuk itself works like any other sukuk, as covered in the [Oct 7 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-07.html). The investor owns a share in real assets, and the return comes from those assets. The green label adds one rule: the money must reach the named projects.

Three checks keep the label honest. First, the issuer publishes a framework listing the kinds of projects the money can fund. Second, an independent firm reviews that framework before the sukuk is sold and gives a second opinion. Third, the issuer reports every year on where the money went and what it achieved, such as megawatts of solar power built or tonnes of emissions avoided. If the money goes elsewhere, the label fails.

Indonesia came first. In March 2018 the government issued the world's first sovereign green sukuk, $1.25 billion for five years at 3.75%, using wakala. The order book ran to 2.5 times the amount on offer, and buyers came from Islamic institutions, Asia, the USA and Europe. The programme has raised about $9.6 billion since 2018. In 2019 Indonesia sold a retail green sukuk to its own citizens, the first of its kind, so an ordinary person could fund the same projects with a small amount.

The market has grown fast since then. Green and other sustainability sukuk reached $23.8 billion of new issuance in 2025, up 54% on the year before, and $73.2 billion was outstanding at the end of the year. The risk for an ordinary investor is greenwashing, a green label on money that funds ordinary spending. The framework, the second opinion and the yearly report are the three papers to read before trusting the label.

For a reader in the USA, green sukuk are hard to buy directly. A USA reader cannot buy Indonesia's green sukuk at a local broker. The closest parallel is a green bond from a development bank, which works the same way except that it pays interest. The three checks, framework, second opinion and yearly report, apply to a green bond too.
