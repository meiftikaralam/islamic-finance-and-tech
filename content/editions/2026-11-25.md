# Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Wednesday, November 25, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. Oman issues its first Islamic Treasury bills

The Central Bank of Oman held its first auction of Government Islamic Treasury Bills on Monday, November 23. Islamic banks bid RO 61 million (about $158.6 million) for the new short-term paper, and RO 38 million (about $98.8 million) will be issued today, November 25, for 91 days at an average accepted profit rate of 4.11%. The bills come in four tenors — 28, 91, 182 and 364 days — and are sold through electronic auctions.

**What to learn:** A treasury bill is a short-term loan to the government. The government borrows money for a few weeks or months and pays it back with a return. An Islamic treasury bill does this without interest: the return is a profit rate agreed at auction, here 4.11% a year for 91 days.

An Islamic bank in Oman ends most days with surplus cash, and until now it had no Shariah-compliant short-term place to put that cash inside Oman. These bills give it one. The government also gains a new way to borrow money for the short term.

The central bank runs an electronic auction. Islamic banks bid the amount they want and the profit rate they will accept. The central bank takes the lowest rates first until it has the amount it wants. This time banks offered RO 61 million and the central bank took RO 38 million, so demand ran about 1.6 times the amount issued. The first bills run 91 days. Later auctions will offer 28, 182 and 364 days.

The Central Bank of Oman runs the auctions for the government. Licensed banks in Oman take part directly, and the central bank says banks may also bring in other investors. An Islamic bank treasurer in Muscat can now place surplus cash for 91 days and earn a return without interest. Over time, an ordinary investor in Oman benefits because his bank can manage its cash better. The [Nov 16 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-16.html) covered how banks borrow cash overnight — these bills give Islamic banks in Oman a short-term option of their own.

### Guidance: is QQQ halal — what a fund screen catches that a stock screen misses

Omar in Texas holds QQQ in his brokerage account. QQQ is the Invesco fund that tracks the Nasdaq-100, the 100 largest non-financial companies on Nasdaq. Zoya screened the fund in November 2026, using holdings from November 16 and ratings checked November 18. Of every $10,000 in QQQ, $6,228 sits in stocks Zoya rates Compliant, $3,125 in stocks it rates Questionable, and $618 in stocks it rates Non-Compliant. The remaining $29 is cash and index futures.

The Compliant share holds Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Micron and Tesla. The Questionable share holds Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Walmart. Five of QQQ's ten largest companies are Questionable. The twenty Non-Compliant companies are smaller holdings. The largest, T-Mobile at 0.85% of the fund, carries debt equal to 68% of its market capitalisation, above the 30% limit in the AAOIFI standards Zoya follows. Marriott fails the business screen and Comcast fails both screens.

Zoya marks a fund Compliant only when every holding passes its screen or the fund has its own Shariah certification. QQQ meets neither condition, so Zoya leaves the fund unrated. That is the difference between screening a stock and screening a fund. A single stock can pass cleanly while the fund holding it still fails, because the fund also holds companies that fail.

Purification does not fix this. Purification means giving to charity the small share of income that came from impermissible sources, and it applies to income from investments that pass screening. The $618 is not income to give away. It is Omar's exposure to companies that fail the screen, and purification is not meant to cover it.

The same check gives a different result for chip funds. Every stock in SMH, the VanEck Semiconductor ETF, passes Zoya's screen, so Zoya marks the fund Compliant. SOXX has 99.1% of its weight in Compliant stocks and one unrated holding. But neither fund is Shariah-certified and neither has a Shariah board. SMH lends its shares for a fee — shares worth about 0.6% of the fund were on loan on September 30, 2026 — and its index has no Shariah screen, so a stock that stops passing can stay in the fund. Zoya puts SMH's purification ratio at 1.13% of dividends.

Omar has three ways to keep the Nasdaq exposure without QQQ itself. Zoya Pro's Nasdaq 20 basket holds the 20 largest Compliant stocks in QQQ and is rebuilt daily so it stays fully Compliant. A custom basket built from QQQ drops the Non-Compliant and Questionable stocks and keeps about 53% of QQQ's weight. Or he can buy a Shariah-certified ETF: HLAL charges 0.50% a year and overlaps QQQ by 53.0%, while SPUS charges 0.45% and overlaps by 50.9%, as the [Nov 22 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-22.html) shows. Certified funds can still hold companies Zoya rates Questionable, such as Microsoft, because their boards apply the standards differently.
