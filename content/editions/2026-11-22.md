### Guidance: what the 11 US halal ETFs cost and who checks them

An investor in Chicago has $10,000 to put into a halal ETF. Eleven such funds trade on US exchanges as of November 2026. Seven track an index. Four are actively managed, which means a manager picks the stocks.

The first number to compare is the fee. An ETF charges a percentage of the money in the fund every year, called the expense ratio. The eleven funds charge between 0.40% and 0.91% a year. On $10,000, the cheapest fund, Manzil's MNZL at 0.40%, costs $40 a year. The priciest, Saturna's Amana Developing World ETF at 0.91%, costs $91 a year. The two largest funds sit near the cheap end. SPUS, the largest at $3.37 billion, charges 0.45%, or $45 a year on $10,000. HLAL, the second largest at $946 million, charges 0.50%.

The second number is what the fund holds. SPUS, HLAL and MNZL all hold US stocks. MNZL holds about 490 stocks, more than SPUS or HLAL, because it also includes mid-sized companies. SPWO holds stocks from around the world. SPTE holds technology stocks. SPRE holds property companies. SPSK is the only fund that holds sukuk instead of shares, and it paid out 4.95% in distributions over the last twelve months. Wahed's UMMA holds about 100 international stocks. Saturna's three Amana funds are stock-picked: one for growth, one for dividend income, and one for emerging markets. The Amana funds are also the smallest, with the largest holding $9.65 million.

The third check is who watches the fund. Every fund names its checker. SP Funds uses Ratings Intelligence Partners, with Raqaba as its Shariah adviser. HLAL is screened by Yasaar, with the Shariyah Review Bureau as adviser. UMMA is certified by the Shariyah Review Bureau. MNZL uses IdealRatings and a three-member supervisory board. Saturna picks the Amana funds itself, and Amanie Advisors reviews them every quarter.

The checkers do not all use the same rules. SPUS measures a company's debt against its average market value over 36 months, with a limit of 33%. HLAL measures debt against total assets, with a limit of 33.33%. UMMA applies the AAOIFI limits of 30%. The same stock can sit inside one fund and outside another that sounds identical. The [Nov 19 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-19.html) shows how that happens, using Anthropic's first screening: one screener divided by market value and another by total assets, and the two reached different answers.

Returns over the twelve months to November 20, 2026: SPUS gained 3.7% and HLAL gained 5.5%, against 5.2% for the conventional S&P 500 fund SPY. Those figures cover one year only, and the next year can look different. The comparison that holds over time is the fee. A fund that charges 0.91% costs more than twice as much each year as one that charges 0.40%, and the fee comes out whether the fund rises or falls.

For the investor in Chicago, the order of work is plain. Start with the fee, then check what the fund holds, then check who certifies it. She buys the fund through a brokerage account, the same way she buys a share, and the fund's own page lists its current fee and holdings.

### Guidance: what happens when a halal home finance payment is late

A homeowner in Texas pays $2,000 a month under a Guidance home purchase plan. One month the payment arrives 20 days late. The plan itself works as covered in the [Nov 21 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-21.html): she co-owns the house with Guidance and buys a slice of its share each month.

In a conventional loan, a late fee of 4 to 5% would add $80 to $100 to the bill. The lender keeps that fee. Interest also keeps accruing on the overdue amount, so the balance is higher the next month.

A halal contract works on a fixed amount. The sale price or the rent was agreed at the start, and time passing cannot make it grow. A charge that grows a debt because of delay is riba, the same increase the Quran forbids. AAOIFI's standard on the defaulting debtor says no extra charge may be put on a late debt. A provider may charge a fixed amount only to cover its real collection costs, or an amount that goes to charity. It never keeps a late charge as profit. Guidance caps its late fee at $50 and does not profit from it.

The rules above cover a customer who can pay and chooses to wait. A customer who cannot pay because of lost income or illness is in a different position. The standard sets its enforcement measures aside for that case, and the debt still does not grow.

The provider still has ways to protect itself. It can report the missed payment, refuse future finance, and ask a court to recover the original amount. A clause in the contract can also make the remaining instalments due at once if a payment is missed. Scholars differ on the details. Some boards, including in Malaysia, allow compensation for a real, provable loss. The debt itself never grows with time.

For the homeowner in Texas, the late payment costs her at most the capped fee. Her $2,000 payment stays $2,000, and the months she already paid still count toward owning the house.
