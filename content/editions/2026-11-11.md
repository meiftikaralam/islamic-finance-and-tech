### 🌍 1. HiLITE launches a Shariah-certified real estate fund in India

HiLITE Group, a real estate developer from Kozhikode in Kerala, India, is entering investment management with a new arm, HiLITE Investment Management. Its first offering, HiLITE Ethical Fund 1, is a real estate fund of up to Rs 200 crore, about $21 million, registered with India's market regulator SEBI as a Category II Alternative Investment Fund and certified Shariah-compliant by TASIS. The fund will invest in new and redeveloped property projects, runs for seven years, and asks for a minimum of Rs 1 crore, about $107,000, paid in instalments over three years, with payouts to investors starting from the fourth year.

**What to learn:** An Alternative Investment Fund is a pooled fund in India for wealthy individuals and institutions, regulated by SEBI. Category II is the category for funds that invest in private assets such as real estate and unlisted companies. HiLITE has built property in Kerala for thirty years, including Focus Mall, HiLITE City and the World Trade Center Kozhikode, and it now wants outside investors to share in its projects instead of funding them from its own balance sheet alone. The fund puts money into greenfield projects, which are built from scratch on empty land, and brownfield projects, which redevelop existing sites. Each deal is first checked by HiLITE against the Shariah guidelines set by TASIS, and then reviewed by TASIS's own Shariah advisory team. Building takes two to four years, so the fund expects to start paying investors from the fourth year, and the fund closes after seven years. The people behind it are HiLITE Group chairman P. Sulaiman and chief executive Ajil Muhammed. PwC audits the fund, Mitcon Credentia acts as trustee, SBI is the banker, Nuvama is the custodian, and Ajmal & Company advises on regulation. The minimum of Rs 1 crore, about $107,000, puts this fund out of reach of an ordinary investor. It is built for wealthy individuals and institutions. For an ordinary investor in the USA, the accessible routes into the same kind of asset are different: a Shariah-compliant real estate fund or a sukuk fund bought through a brokerage, where the minimum is the price of one share or unit. For a Muslim investor in Kerala with money set aside, the fund offers a professionally managed route into property returns without interest. The trade-off is the size of the ticket and the wait: the money stays in the fund for years while the buildings go up.

### How an ordinary investor can buy sukuk

Sukuk issues are often oversubscribed several times over, yet ordinary investors hold very little of them. The buyers in the order book are mostly institutions. The Islamic Development Bank publishes who buys its sukuk. Its $1 billion issue in May 2026 drew orders of more than $2.65 billion. Central banks and official institutions took 49%, banks and private banks took 42%, and fund managers took 9%. There was no line for individual investors at all. When Dubai Islamic Bank sold a $1 billion sukuk in June 2026, the book passed $2.3 billion, and banks and private banks took 77%, with fund managers on 21%. In Malaysia, banks hold 42.9% of government securities, pension funds hold 41.6% and insurers hold 7.9%. Companies, government agencies and individuals together hold 2.9%. In Indonesia, where the government has sold retail sukuk since 2009, individuals hold 8.4% of all government securities.

Ordinary investors are kept out by the size of the lots. A $1 billion Sharjah sukuk in 2018 had a minimum of $200,000. In Malaysia, standard lots were RM10 million for government paper and RM5 million for corporate paper. When institutions already cover a book two or three times, the issuer has little reason to cut the lots smaller. Even when the door opened, investors did not always come through it. In 2014, a Malaysian retail sukuk of RM100 million drew only 400 applications from the public, worth RM3.95 million, while placement agents applied for the full amount.

What worked was a small ticket, a short tenor and a sale inside the app people already use. Indonesia sells retail sukuk from Rp1 million, online, through apps people already have, and one series in 2026 drew 93,548 investors. Malaysia's Sukuk Prihatin was sold inside online banking and filled its book. Bangladesh set aside 5% of its sukuk for individuals in 2020, with a minimum of Tk10,000, and now reserves 10%. Indonesia's ST017 retail savings sukuk, covered in the [Nov 3 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-03.html), follows the same pattern: a small starting amount of IDR 1 million and a return paid monthly.

For a reader in the USA, there is no retail sukuk market to buy into directly. The practical routes are a sukuk fund or ETF bought through a brokerage, such as the SP Funds Dow Jones Global Sukuk ETF, which holds a portfolio of dollar sukuk, or a Shariah-compliant income fund. The minimum is the price of one share. One more point: an oversubscribed book counts the size of the orders placed. It does not count how many people wanted the sukuk. Investors often place orders larger than what they truly want because they expect to be scaled back.
