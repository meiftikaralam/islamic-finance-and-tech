# Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Friday, November 13, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. A €660 million Islamic lease will fund a new rail crossing in Istanbul

The Islamic Development Bank and Türkiye signed a €660 million financing agreement, about $730 million at today's rate of about 1.11 dollars to the euro, on November 12 for the Istanbul North Rail Crossing Project, a 127-kilometre electrified railway. The deal is the first "forward lease" agreement between the bank and Türkiye, and the bank's largest sovereign financing for the country. It was signed at COP31 in Antalya, where development banks announced about $5.1 billion of financing for 10 projects. Türkiye's finance minister Mehmet Şimşek spoke at the signing ceremony.

**What to learn:** A forward lease is a lease agreed before the asset is ready. It is a form of ijara, the lease contract covered in the [Oct 16 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-16.html). The core point is the order of the payments.

The bank pays for the asset first. The Islamic Development Bank puts up the €660 million, and the railway is built with that money. The bank owns the asset as it is built, and the risk of delivery sits with the bank. The contract describes the railway in full detail: the route, the 127 kilometres of electrified track, the standards it must meet. If the finished railway does not match that description, or is not ready by the agreed date, Türkiye can refuse it.

While the railway is being built, Türkiye pays a fixed amount each month. No interest is charged on these payments. They are not rent yet, because there is no railway to use. They are advance rent, held on account. The bank earns nothing from them during the building years.

When the railway is finished and handed over, the lease starts. From that day, Türkiye pays rent for using the line. The advance rent paid during construction is set against the rent bills, so the same period is not paid for twice. If the railway is never delivered, the contract ends and the advance rent goes back to Türkiye.

Türkiye's transport ministry builds and runs the railway. The people it touches are the exporters moving goods between Europe and Asia, and the passengers crossing between the two continents. The 127 kilometres of electrified track should move freight and passengers faster and with lower emissions than road transport. It is the first forward lease between the bank and Türkiye, and the same structure can now be used on other big projects. The bank has approved more than $16.5 billion of financing for Türkiye across 556 operations.

### 🌍 2. The UAE's central bank puts Islamic finance under one rulebook

The UAE central bank has issued Regulation No. 1/2026, a detailed rulebook for Islamic financial institutions and takaful operators. The rules cover the main Islamic contracts, murabaha, ijarah, salam, istisna'a, mudaraba, musharaka and wakala, plus Islamic deposits, investment accounts, guarantees and takaful products. They apply to Islamic banks and finance companies, conventional banks running Islamic windows, and takaful operators. Firms may need to review existing deals and documents to check they meet the new rules. Seya Rahnema of law firm Pinsent Masons said the rules should give the market more legal certainty and more consistent documentation.

**What to learn:** Shariah governance is the system that checks whether a bank's products follow Islamic rules. Each Islamic bank has a Shariah board of scholars that reviews products before launch. The central bank now adds its own layer: detailed rules for how each contract must be written and documented.

Until now, the principles guided transactions, and each bank applied them in its own way. The new regulation turns those principles into written rules the central bank supervises. A product sold as Shariah-compliant must now meet the same written rules at every bank.

A bank that wants to offer a murabaha home purchase plan must show the asset, the sale, and who carries the risk in its documents. The Shariah board checks the product. The regulator can now review how the bank did its checks. If the documents do not match the rules, the bank must fix them. Earlier in 2026, Dubai's financial free zone regulator widened its own Islamic finance rules so more Shariah-compliant securities and funds fall under its oversight.

The work for banks starts with paper. Every existing contract and product document has to be read against the new rules, and where a document does not match, it has to be fixed. That takes lawyers, compliance staff and Shariah scholars, and it costs money. Pinsent Masons says firms may need legal and regulatory advice to do the work. Smaller finance companies feel the cost more than the big banks, because they run the same checks with smaller teams. New products can also slow down, because a bank may hold a launch until its documents pass the new rules.

The central bank has to check that every bank follows the rules. It must review products across Islamic banks, finance companies, Islamic windows and takaful operators, and read each rule the same way for all of them. If two banks read a rule in two different ways, the central bank has to settle what the rule means.

The first people it touches are a Muslim in Dubai opening an Islamic account, and a small business owner taking Islamic financing. Their products now sit under one written rulebook. In the USA, there is no single rulebook like this. Islamic banks there follow state and federal banking rules, and their Shariah boards check the products.

### Diminishing musharaka: buying a home together with the bank, a share at a time

A diminishing musharaka is a way to buy a home without a loan. The bank and the buyer buy the home together. The buyer pays rent on the bank's share, and buys a small piece of the bank's share each month. Over time the bank's share shrinks and the buyer's share grows, until the buyer owns the home outright.

Take a family in the USA buying a home for $300,000. They put in $60,000, which is 20% of the price. The bank puts in $240,000, which is 80%. The family moves in and pays two things each month. First, rent on the bank's share, set from the market rent for a similar home. Second, a payment that buys a slice of the bank's share. Say the family buys $1,000 of the bank's share in the first month. The bank's share falls to $239,000, so the rent for the next month is slightly lower. Each month the family owns a little more and rents a little less. After the last payment, the bank owns nothing and the family owns the whole home.

The bank earns rent because it owns part of a real home. The bank also shares the ownership risk. If the home loses value, both sides lose. The structure is used for homes in the USA, the UK and Australia, and for business equipment too. A buyer in the USA can find it through Islamic home finance providers that offer purchase plans on this model.
