# Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Monday, November 23, 2026

## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. Tamara raises $400m to fund interest-free instalments

Tamara, a Saudi buy now, pay later company, has raised $400 million in new funding from two global banks. MUFG of Japan is providing SAR 749 million, about $200 million, and Standard Chartered is adding $200 million to an existing facility. The funding is asset-backed, which means it is tied to the instalment payments Tamara's shoppers owe. Tamara serves more than 26 million customers and over 160,000 merchants across Saudi Arabia and the Gulf. Its co-founder and chief executive, Abdulmajeed Alsukhan, said the backing shows growing international trust in the region's financial services.

**What to learn:** Tamara lets a shopper split a purchase into monthly payments with no interest. The shopper pays the store price and nothing more. The store gets paid in full by Tamara within days. That leaves Tamara waiting for the shopper's monthly payments, and its cash is locked up until those payments arrive.

Asset-backed financing unlocks that cash. Tamara gathers thousands of shoppers' future payments into one pool and borrows against the pool from MUFG and Standard Chartered. The banks' money is tied to real purchases that already happened, and the shoppers' repayments flow back to the banks. Tamara says the MUFG part is a first: no international bank has given a fintech in the region funding in Saudi riyals through this kind of structure before. Funding in riyals matters because Tamara's shoppers pay in riyals, so the money coming in matches the money going out.

For the shopper in Riyadh, the effect is plain. More funding lets Tamara offer instalments to more shoppers and more stores. For the USA, the picture is different. American buy now, pay later firms such as Afterpay and Klarna fund themselves the same way, through pools of customer payments, but their longer plans can carry interest and late fees. Tamara's version keeps the shopper's price fixed and charges no late fees.

## Learn

### Guidance: buy now, pay later, the halal way

A shopper in Riyadh buys a washing machine for SAR 1,200, about $320. At checkout she splits it into four monthly payments of SAR 300, about $80 each. The price never changes. The store gets the full SAR 1,200 from Tamara within days, and Tamara collects the four payments from the shopper.

Tamara earns its money from the store. The store pays Tamara a percentage of each sale. Published guides put that cut at roughly 4 to 7 percent of the order, though Tamara does not publish a fixed rate. The store accepts the cut because shoppers buy more when they can split the payment.

The shopper pays no interest and no late fee. Tamara's site states no late fees. If a payment arrives late, the amount she owes stays the same, because a debt cannot grow with time. The [Nov 22 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-22.html) explains the rule behind that, and the AAOIFI standard it rests on.

Tamara does not operate in the USA. It runs in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Bahrain. An American shopper who wants the same idea has the conventional versions, Afterpay and Klarna, and their longer plans can charge interest.

### How an AI assistant checks a stock for you

An investor in New Jersey asks Claude, an AI assistant, whether a stock is halal. Until now the assistant answered from its own training data, which can be out of date or plain wrong. Zoya, the screening app, launched a connector on November 12 that changes where the answer comes from.

The connector uses the Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets an AI assistant call an outside service and read its live data. Once connected, the assistant pulls Zoya's compliance report for the stock: the business screen, the debt and interest-income ratios, and the verdict. It reads the same report a Zoya user sees in the app.

The [Nov 19 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-19.html) showed why that matters. Two screeners can reach different answers on the same company because they divide by different numbers. An answer that cites the report shows its working, so the investor can check the numbers behind the verdict.

The connector works with ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, VS Code and Codex. It is open to Zoya Pro subscribers and developers on a paid Zoya API plan. The assistant still gives Zoya's reading of the rules. A scholar's ruling is still the final word for the investor.
