### 🌍 1. Pakistan marketplace Zarea plans a Rs3 billion rights issue to repay its sukuk and buy more stock

Zarea Limited, a Lahore-based online marketplace for farm and industrial commodities, plans to raise Rs3 billion (roughly $10.8 million) by issuing 120 million new shares at Rs25 each to its existing shareholders. The board approved the plan on October 30, and the draft offer document went up on the company and Pakistan Stock Exchange websites on November 2. About Rs2 billion (roughly $7.2 million) of the money will repay a short-term sukuk issued on October 6, and about Rs952 million (roughly $3.4 million) of the net proceeds will fund extra working capital for buying commodities. The total working-capital need is Rs1.37 billion (roughly $4.9 million), with the rest from the company's own cash. The sukuk carries profit at six-month KIBOR plus 2.50% a year, falls due on April 5, 2027, and its estimated Rs148 million (roughly $0.53 million) profit will be paid from the company's own cash.

**What to learn:** Zarea runs a digital marketplace where buyers and sellers trade farm produce and industrial goods such as grain, cement, and steel. A trader listing grain on the platform needs the company to hold stock ready for buyers, and holding stock takes cash. That cash need is why the company borrowed through a short-term sukuk in October. A sukuk is a certificate tied to real assets or a real business activity, and the return comes from that activity rather than from interest. Zarea's second sukuk uses a Wakala structure, which was explained in the [Oct 19 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-19.html). Under Wakala, an agent invests or manages funds on the investors' behalf under agreed terms. The sukuk was fully taken up by institutional investors and rated A-1, the top short-term grade. The rights issue is the next step in the same funding chain. Instead of borrowing again, Zarea will ask its existing shareholders for fresh equity, offering 16 new shares for every 35 shares they already hold. Shareholders who take up their rights put in new cash at Rs25 (about 9 cents) a share, a price set about 29% below the market price. The change for a farmer or trader using the platform is practical. Repaying the sukuk on time keeps the company's funding record clean, and the extra working capital lets it buy and hold more stock across its procurement hubs. In the USA, a small listed company in the same position also raises equity from existing shareholders through a rights offering, and a Shariah-compliant borrower there faces the same choice between new shares and new debt.

### 🌍 2. Fitch cuts Indonesia's postal sukuk to default after a missed payment with no cure

Fitch Ratings has downgraded PT Pos Indonesia's Tranche I Sukuk to D(idn), its default grade, after the state-owned postal company missed a scheduled cash flow payment and no cure or resolution followed. The downgrade was reported on November 3. Pos Indonesia runs the national postal and logistics service, with offices and agents across the country. The company has said it will hold meetings with bondholders and sukuk holders in November 2026 to discuss restructuring, and its parent group Danantara has said the company will be reorganised as a logistics holding company and will not be put into bankruptcy.

**What to learn:** A credit rating is an agency's public judgement of how likely a borrower is to repay, and D is the grade that means a payment has been missed and not fixed. The rating scale and what D means were explained in the [Nov 2 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-02.html), when Zetrix's rating fell to one step above default. The new part here is how a missed payment turns into a default grade. Sukuk documents usually allow a short grace period after a due date, often 14 business days, during which the issuer can still pay and avoid default. Fitch moves the rating to D when that window closes with the payment still unpaid and no agreed cure in place. The payment at issue here is an ijarah return, which is the rental-style return on a sukuk backed by leased assets. The people in the process are the issuer, which owes the payment, the rating agency, which judges the risk, and the sukuk holders, who hold the right to be paid. The change for a sukuk holder is a shift from waiting for scheduled returns to waiting for a restructuring proposal at the November meetings. For a postal customer, the operating question is separate from the funding question. The company keeps delivering mail and parcels while its debts are reorganised, and Danantara's statement rules out bankruptcy. In the USA, a bondholder in the same position watches the same sequence, with a grace period, a default rating, and then a meeting with the issuer about new terms.

### 🌍 3. Indonesia opens its ST017 retail savings sukuk on November 6, starting from IDR 1 million

Indonesia's government will open its ST017 Savings Sukuk to retail investors on November 6, 2026, with subscriptions running to December 2 and settlement on December 9. The schedule is tentative, pending the government's final announcement. The offering comes in two versions: ST017T2, a two-year tenor maturing on November 10, 2028, and ST017T4, a four-year tenor maturing on November 10, 2030. Anyone can start with IDR 1 million (roughly $60). The return uses a floating-with-floor mechanism, so the rate can rise with the central bank benchmark rate but cannot fall below a minimum set at issuance. That minimum has not been announced yet, and the first return payment is scheduled for January 10, 2027.

**What to learn:** A savings sukuk is a government sukuk sold directly to ordinary citizens in small amounts. The government raises money from the public, and the public receives a regular return from a Shariah-compliant structure rather than from interest. The floating-with-floor mechanism is the part that needs explaining. The return moves up when the Bank Indonesia benchmark rate moves up, and the floor guarantees a minimum return even if the benchmark falls. The previous series, ST016, paid 6.05% a year on its two-year tenor and 6.25% on its four-year tenor, which gives a sense of the level without predicting the ST017 rate. A savings sukuk differs from a retail sukuk in one practical way. A retail sukuk can be sold on to another investor on the secondary market, while a savings sukuk cannot be traded or transferred. The trade-off for that lock-in is an early redemption option, which lets the holder withdraw up to 50% of the investment early. The people in the process are the finance ministry, which issues the sukuk, the distribution partners such as banks and securities firms, which take subscriptions, and the ordinary investor, who buys from IDR 1 million. For an investor setting aside small amounts each month, the change is a place to park savings that follows Shariah rules, pays monthly, and returns the principal at maturity. In the USA, an ordinary investor in the same position uses a savings bond or a bank savings account, which pay interest and are therefore not Shariah-compliant.

## Learn

### Rights issues

A rights issue is a way for a company that already trades on a stock exchange to raise fresh cash from the people who already own its shares. The company offers its existing shareholders the right to buy new shares, usually at a price below the current market price. Each shareholder gets rights in proportion to the shares they already hold, so a shareholder with more shares gets the right to buy more new shares.

The process runs in clear steps. The board approves the issue and sets the price and the ratio. Zarea, covered in story 1 above, set a ratio of 16 new shares for every 35 shares held, at Rs25 per share. Shareholders then decide whether to take up their rights and pay for the new shares, sell their rights to someone else where the rules allow, or let the rights lapse. If a shareholder takes up the rights in full, their percentage ownership of the company stays the same, because every shareholder had the same chance to buy. If a shareholder does not take up the rights, their percentage ownership falls, because new shares go to others. That fall is called dilution.

A company chooses a rights issue when it needs cash for a specific purpose and wants to give its current owners the first chance to supply it. Zarea needs cash to repay a sukuk and to fund working capital, so it turned to shareholders before borrowing again. For a beginner, the point to remember is simple. A rights issue is an invitation, with a deadline and a set price, and the decision is whether the purpose is worth putting in more cash at that price.
