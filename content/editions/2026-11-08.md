## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. Ominvest raises $260 million with a sukuk that never matures

Oman International Development and Investment Company (Ominvest) raised OMR 100 million (about $260 million) through a private placement of perpetual sukuk, and demand ran at twice the amount on offer. The Sukuk Al Mudaraba Trust Certificates pay an initial profit rate of 6.50% a year, paid every three months, and have no fixed maturity date. More than 90 investors took part, including pension funds, government-related institutions, banks, asset managers and wealthy individuals. Alizz Islamic Bank and Ubhar Capital ran the issue. The money comes ahead of December 13, 2026, when $143 million of sukuk Ominvest issued in 2021 reaches its first call date and can be redeemed. Ominvest's chairman, Rashad Muhammad Al Zubair, said perpetual capital gives the group flexibility across market cycles. Group chief executive Abdul Aziz Mohammed Al Balushi said the 6.50% rate showed investor confidence despite difficult global financing conditions. The company's board has also approved Ominvest taking part as a strategic investor in the proposed merger of Alizz Islamic Bank with Bank Nizwa, subject to regulatory approval.

**What to learn:** A perpetual sukuk has no maturity date. The issuer pays profit at set intervals and never has to repay the principal on a fixed date. The holder keeps receiving profit for as long as the issuer keeps the sukuk outstanding. Ominvest's new sukuk pays 6.50% a year, every three months. A call date is a date written into the sukuk when it is first sold. On that date, the issuer can choose to pay the holders back and close the sukuk early. The holder cannot force this; it is the issuer's choice. Ominvest's 2021 sukuk reaches its first call date on December 13, 2026, and the new issue raises the money to pay those holders back. Rank is the order in which people get paid if the issuer runs into trouble. Senior lenders are paid first. Perpetual sukuk holders are paid after them, and shareholders are paid last. That is what "rank below ordinary debt" means. The higher profit rate of 6.50% is the extra return for taking that lower place in the payment order. Permanent capital is money the company can keep with no date to pay it back. Ominvest needs it because its businesses — insurance, banking, real estate, private equity and technology — hold assets for years. A fixed repayment date could force it to sell assets or borrow again at a bad time to find the cash. The structure here is Mudaraba, a partnership where investors supply the capital and Ominvest manages it, and the profit is shared on the terms set in the agreement. The buyers include pension funds, so the profit payments flow through to retirement savings.

### Halal savings accounts in the USA

A conventional savings account pays interest, and most scholars treat that interest as riba. A halal savings account pays a share of the profit the bank earns from Shariah-compliant financing instead. The difference is what the bank does with the money. A conventional bank lends the deposit out at interest and pays the depositor a fixed rate, no matter how the loans perform. A halal bank uses the deposit to finance real things — homes, business equipment and commercial property — through Shariah-compliant contracts, and shares the profit from those deals with the depositor. The rate is only expected, not promised. Three FDIC-insured US banks offer one nationwide, according to research by Zoya checked on November 6, 2026. Stearns Bank runs Salaam Banking, with a $50 minimum to open and the only published rate sheet of the three. University Bank runs UIF, with a $100 minimum, monthly profit payments and no account fees. Bank of Whittier calls itself a riba-free bank, with branches in California and Texas and no published rates. All three carry FDIC insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank. The expected profit rate can change, and the actual profit can be higher, lower or zero. Each of the three has Shariah oversight: Stearns has a three-scholar board, UIF has a board that publishes signed fatwas and audit reports, and Bank of Whittier publishes annual Shariah audits. Some apps marketed as halal savings are not bank deposits at all. Wahed's Everyday Shariah Account is an investment account that can lose value, and Goldsand's own terms warn users they can lose their whole deposit. A balance in one of those can fall, so it is not a place for money you cannot afford to lose.
