### 🇺🇸 1. A flat fee Islamic adviser launches in California

ShariaWiz Advisory launched on November 6 in Folsom, California. The firm is a California registered investment adviser for Muslim Americans. It brings financial planning, investment management, and Islamic estate planning tools together in one service, and it charges a flat annual fee. The chief executive is Shakeb Kundiwala. The team also includes Abed Awad, an attorney who works on Islamic law and estate planning, and Shaykh Umer Khan, a Shariah and Islamic finance scholar.

The fee is $3,000 a year for an individual and $6,000 a year for a married couple. The fee stays the same whatever the size of the portfolio, and there is no minimum amount a client must invest first. The fee includes access to Islamic wills, trusts, and related estate planning tools through its affiliate, ShariaWiz, Inc. The firm starts in California and plans to expand into Texas, New York, New Jersey, and Illinois in the first quarter of 2027, subject to registration in each state.

**What to learn:** Most advisers charge a percentage of the money they manage. The charge grows as the portfolio grows, even when the work stays much the same. A flat fee works the other way. The client pays a set amount each year, and the amount stays fixed as the portfolio rises or falls.

The maths runs in steps. Take a portfolio of $200,000. At 1% a year, the adviser charges $2,000. The flat fee of $3,000 costs more at this size. Take a portfolio of $300,000. At 1%, the charge is $3,000. The two charges are equal here. Take a portfolio of $1,000,000. At 1%, the charge is $10,000. The flat fee is $3,000 for an individual, which is $7,000 less for that year. For a married couple paying $6,000, the two charges are equal at a portfolio of $600,000.

The flat fee also changes what the adviser is paid to do. A percentage fee pays more when the client keeps more money invested with the adviser. A flat fee pays the same amount whether the client invests, pays off a home financing, or gives money to family. The advice can cover the whole plan, and in this firm the plan includes zakat, charitable giving, and Islamic inheritance planning alongside the investments.

The people this touches are Muslim families in the USA who want advice that follows their faith and who had to use separate professionals before: one for investments, one for a will, and one for tax questions. A family can now buy those parts together from one firm. The trade-off is the fee itself. A family with a small portfolio pays $3,000 for advice that a percentage adviser would give for less, and product costs and custodial charges are charged on top of the advisory fee.

A reader who is comparing advisers in the USA has three broad choices. A percentage adviser charges about 1% of the portfolio each year. A flat fee adviser charges a set amount, as this firm does. A halal robo adviser or a Shariah ETF, such as Wahed's HLAL, charges a lower percentage and gives no personal plan. HalalWallet, which tracks the USA market, counted 167 consumer products from 43 providers across eight categories as of November 16, and only 56 of those products carry a published Shariah document. The contract and the Shariah document are the papers to read before paying any of the three.

### How banks borrow cash overnight, and what interest-free banks do instead

A bank's cash moves every day. Deposits come in, withdrawals go out, and payments settle. A bank can end the day short of cash even when it is healthy. It needs a way to borrow a small amount for one night, and a place to put spare cash so it earns something.

In the conventional system, banks lend to each other overnight in the interbank market. A bank with spare cash lends to a bank that is short, and the borrower pays interest for the night. The central bank also runs two windows, called standing facilities. A bank can place spare cash with the central bank overnight and earn interest, or borrow from the central bank against collateral and pay interest. These tools keep the whole system running day to day.

Interest-free banks could not use any of this, because every tool ran on interest. That left them holding cash that earned nothing, and with no backstop when they ran short. Ethiopia's central bank, the National Bank of Ethiopia, has now fixed that gap. On November 12 it issued two directives, EPD/01/2026 and EPD/02/2026, giving the country's interest-free banks their own versions of the same tools.

The first directive covers the central bank's own windows. An interest-free bank can place surplus cash with the central bank and get it back by 10:00 AM the next business day. It can also borrow from the central bank the same day by pledging collateral. The central bank can issue its own certificates, at Birr 1 million (about $6,200) each, that only interest-free banks can trade between themselves.

The second directive sets up an interest-free interbank market. Banks with spare cash post offers showing the amount, the expected profit rate and the term. Banks that are short pick the best offer. Deals run overnight or for seven days, with a minimum size of Birr 25 million (about $155,000), and trading runs from 8:30 AM to 4:00 PM on an electronic platform.

Both directives rest on wakala, the agency contract covered in the [Oct 19 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-19.html). The bank with cash appoints the bank that needs it as its agent to invest the money. The return is quoted as an expected profit rate, and it is a target, not a promise. If the investment earns the target or more, the lender gets its money plus the expected profit, and the agent keeps any extra as a reward. If it earns less, the lender gets what was actually earned. If it loses money and the agent was not at fault, the lender bears the loss. As an example, Birr 100 million placed for seven days at a 12% expected profit rate earns about Birr 230,800 (about $1,430) if the target is met.

The people this touches are the customers of Ethiopia's interest-free banks, such as Hijra Bank and ZamZam Bank in Addis Ababa. Their banks can now manage cash without interest, which should let them hold less idle money and compete more evenly with conventional banks. One gap remains: there are still no sukuk for these banks to hold, so their investment choices stay narrow. In the USA, the Federal Reserve's overnight tools also run on interest, so American Islamic banks cannot use them either.

### The AI underwriter: who reads your application, and who decides

An underwriter is the person at a finance company who reads an application and decides whether the company should say yes, say no, or ask for more proof. An AI underwriter is software that does the reading and the first checks. A person still makes the final decision.

Take a bakery owner in Michigan. She applies for $50,000 of murabaha financing to buy a new oven. In a murabaha deal the finance company buys the oven and sells it to her at a stated price, and she pays that price in instalments. She sends in her bank statements, her tax return, and a quote for the oven.

The software reads those papers first. It pulls out the numbers it needs, such as her yearly sales, her costs, and the price on the oven quote. It then checks whether the numbers agree with each other. Sales on the tax return should match the money going into the bank account, allowing for cash sales and timing. If a paper is missing, or two papers give different figures, the software flags the file for a person to look at.

The software then does the maths in steps. Say her papers show $240,000 of sales in a year. Her costs are $200,000 for the same year. $240,000 minus $200,000 leaves $40,000 of profit for the year. $40,000 divided by 12 is about $3,333 of profit a month. The software compares that monthly figure with the monthly instalment on the $50,000 deal. A deal that takes most of her monthly profit leaves her with no room when sales fall, so the file needs a closer look.

An Islamic finance company needs one more check, and the software does that check before the decision. It looks for interest in the contract, it checks whether the business works in a prohibited sector, and it checks whether the deal matches the structure named in the papers. A murabaha file should show a real oven, a real purchase by the finance company, and a sale price stated at the start. CXingularity, a lending software company based in Dubai, sells a system that does this Shariah check inside its underwriting software. Its Halal module screens for interest and prohibited sectors, checks murabaha deals, and maps its checks to AAOIFI and IFSB standards for review by a Shariah board. The company reports that lenders using its system have underwritten more than $600 million of SME loans and reached decisions on more than 15,000 small businesses in the 24 months to 2026, with a pre-scored decision in under 20 minutes. Those figures are company reported, and they cover the whole system, not only the Halal module.

The software then writes a short memo. The memo lists what it found, what it could not confirm, and the score it gave the file. The human underwriter reads the memo, looks at the flagged papers, and decides. CXingularity states this limit in plain terms on its own site: the company prepares the evidence, the scores, and the recommendations, and approval stays with the lender and its credit committee.

The limits matter for an ordinary investor and for a small business owner. Software reads papers fast, and it applies the same checks to every file. It can also misread a scanned page, miss context that a person would catch, or score a good business badly because its papers are thin. A new business with strong orders and weak paperwork is the case most likely to need a person. That is why the safe setup keeps two separate checks in human hands: the credit decision, and the Shariah board review of the rules the software follows.

For a business in the USA, this is the direction Islamic finance companies are moving in. A small provider cannot hire a large underwriting team. Software that reads the papers and does the first checks lets a small team handle more applications, and its time goes to the flagged files. The decision still needs a named person who can explain it, and a Shariah board that has approved the rules behind it.
