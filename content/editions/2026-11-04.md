## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. IILM sets a new record with a $1.572 billion short-term sukuk auction

The International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation (IILM) has completed its largest ever short-term sukuk auction, raising $1.572 billion across five tenors on November 3. The auction drew a total order book of $3.772 billion, and the final amount was upsized by $100 million from an initial target of $1.472 billion. The five tranches run from two weeks to twelve months, with profit rates from 4.20% on the two-week tenor to 4.80% on the twelve-month tenor. The auction lifts IILM's outstanding sukuk portfolio to an all-time high of $7.59 billion, and its issuance so far in 2026 now totals $27.352 billion across 103 series, against $22.9 billion across 42 series for the whole of 2025.

**What to learn:** IILM is an international organisation set up by central banks and monetary authorities to give Islamic financial institutions a place to manage short-term cash in a Shariah-compliant way. The problem it solves starts with the treasurer of an Islamic bank who holds cash that will be needed in a few weeks or months. A conventional bank parks that cash in interest-bearing deposits or treasury bills, and an Islamic bank cannot use those instruments. IILM's short-term sukuk fills that gap. The sukuk are issued in tenors from two weeks to twelve months, so a bank can match the tenor to the date it needs the cash back. The auction works through a network of 16 primary dealers, including banks from the Gulf, Malaysia, and Standard Chartered, which distribute the sukuk to Islamic banks, asset managers, and conventional investors. The record size matters because it shows demand from those buyers holding up in a volatile market, with orders more than double the amount on offer. The change for an ordinary investor is indirect. When Islamic banks have a reliable short-term home for their cash, they can run their daily operations smoothly, and that daily stability sits behind the accounts and financing those banks offer. In the USA, a bank treasurer in the same position uses treasury bills or money market instruments that pay interest, so no US equivalent of IILM exists for Shariah-compliant short-term cash.

The money will scale the platform's AI-based origination and underwriting along the trade corridor linking the Greater Bay Area, Oman, and the UAE. mamun's system assesses the company behind a trade as well as the shipment itself, reading the contract, the buyer, the trade documents, and the trading history between the parties, and then lets investors on the platform finance the gap between shipment and payment. The platform is licensed by Oman's Financial Services Authority under its crowdfunding framework, with entities in Abu Dhabi and Hong Kong alongside its Muscat base.

**What to learn:** Trade finance covers the wait between shipping goods and getting paid, and that wait is the problem this story is about. An exporter ships goods today and is often paid 60 to 90 days later, while banks frequently decline to bridge that wait because a small exporter has no long financial track record. mamun's answer changes the basis of the decision. Its system reads the trade itself, including who the buyer is, what the contract says, whether the documents are in order, and whether the two parties have traded reliably before, and judges the risk from that record rather than from the exporter's loan history. Investors on the platform then finance the gap, so the exporter receives cash sooner and the investor earns a return from a real trade. The licence matters to how far the platform can reach. Oman's crowdfunding framework lets mamun bring in investors from inside and outside Oman and finance companies on both sides, subject to the rules of each investor's country. The scale of the gap is large. The Asian Development Bank puts the global trade finance gap at $2.5 trillion, with 41% of small business trade finance applications rejected, and its January 2026 survey found 84% of banks already apply AI to trade-finance risk. The change for a small exporter is direct. A business that ships goods and waits months for payment can seek financing against that specific shipment, judged on the trade documents, instead of waiting for a bank loan decision based on collateral. In the USA, an exporter in the same position uses conventional trade finance from banks or factors, which charge interest, and no Shariah-compliant, AI-scored trade finance platform of this kind is a standard product at US banks.

## Learn

### Trade finance

Trade finance is the financing that covers the gap between goods being shipped and payment arriving. An exporter sends goods to a buyer, and the buyer often pays 60 to 90 days later. During that wait, the exporter has already paid for production, packing, and shipping, and the cash is tied up in goods that are on their way.

The financing works by tying the money to a specific trade. A financier pays the exporter early, or guarantees the payment, and is repaid when the buyer pays. The documents matter as much as the goods. The contract, the invoice, the shipping papers, and the record of past trades between buyer and seller all show whether the trade is real and whether payment is likely. mamun, covered in the AI Watch section above, uses a system that reads those documents and scores the trade from them.

Trade finance follows Shariah rules when the return comes from a real transaction rather than from interest. The financing can use Murabaha, where the financier buys goods and sells them at a disclosed markup, or Wakala, where an agent manages the financing under agreed terms. Both structures were explained in earlier editions, Murabaha in the [Oct 8 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-08.html) and Wakala in the [Oct 19 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-19.html).

A related idea is factoring, which was covered in the [Oct 26 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-26.html). Factoring is one form of trade finance, in which a business sells its unpaid invoices to a financier at a discount. Trade finance is the wider field, and it also covers financing for imports, guarantees for buyers, and funding tied to a shipment before it leaves.

For a beginner, the point to remember is simple. Trade finance turns a waiting period into a funded period. The exporter gets cash while the goods are in transit, the buyer still pays on the agreed date, and the financier earns from financing a real shipment rather than from lending money at interest.
