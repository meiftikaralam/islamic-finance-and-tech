## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. Four more banks win Islamic banking licences in Uzbekistan

Uzbekistan's central bank has granted Islamic banking licences to four more commercial banks. The licences went to Ipak Yuli Bank, Asia Alliance Bank, Orient Finans Bank and Hayot Bank, under board decisions dated November 18. The banks can now offer services that follow Islamic finance rules, under the country's Islamic banking law passed in March 2026.

**What to learn:** A licence is the regulator's permission to run a type of banking. Open Bank received the first Islamic banking licence in Uzbekistan on October 23, as covered in the [Oct 27 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-27.html). Five banks now hold the licence, and the count matters because one bank is a pilot and five banks is a market opening. A customer who wants Islamic banking can now compare offers from five banks instead of taking whatever one bank offers.

The licence names exactly which operations a bank may run. The six are murabaha, mudaraba, musharaka, ijara, salam and wakala, and the [Oct 27 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-27.html) sets out what each one does. The licence also brings a governance rule. Each bank must run its own Islamic finance council of at least three members.

The regulator has set out where this goes next. It expects at least 10 Islamic banks to be operating by 2030, alongside Islamic windows at three state-owned banks, and the government aims to attract about $1 billion in investment and deposits through Islamic banking between 2026 and 2030. Two full-fledged Islamic banks are planned by 2030.

The people this touches are Uzbek customers who stayed outside the banking system for religious reasons. A survey cited by the country's Muslim Board found that 68% of the population does not use traditional banking services for religious reasons, as covered in the [Oct 27 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-27.html). Ipak Yuli Bank alone serves over 2.5 million clients. A shopkeeper in Tashkent who needs stock finance can now walk into one of five banks and ask for a trade or lease arrangement that follows Islamic rules, instead of having no licensed option at all.

For the USA, the picture is different. There is no separate Islamic banking licence category in the USA, as covered in the [Oct 27 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-27.html). A bank that wants to offer Shariah-compliant products does so under its ordinary bank charter and state law.

### How a new stock gets its first Shariah screening

Anthropic starts trading on Nasdaq today, November 19, under the ticker ANT. An ordinary investor in Ohio who keeps a halal portfolio has to decide on the first day whether the stock passes her Shariah screen.

Zoya, an American screening app, ran the check before trading started. Zoya worked from Anthropic's IPO filing with the SEC, updated through November 17. Zoya's verdict is Non-Compliant, and one check out of three decides it.

A Shariah screening looks at three things. The company's main business must be permissible. Its interest-bearing debt and its interest-bearing investments must each stay under 30% of its market value. Income from impermissible sources, including interest, must stay under 5% of total income.

Anthropic sells AI services, a permissible business, and it passes both size checks. At the middle of its IPO price range, its debt equals 11.6% of its market value and its interest-bearing investments equal 22.2%. Both sit under the 30% limit.

The income check is where it fails. In the twelve months to September 30, Anthropic earned $2.551 billion in interest, most of it on the cash and investments it holds. Its total income for the same twelve months was $37.833 billion. Divide the interest by the total income: $2.551 billion ÷ $37.833 billion = 6.74%. The limit is 5%.

In dollars, 5% of $37.833 billion is about $1.89 billion. Anthropic's interest income passes that line by about $659 million.

One part of the screening depends on a choice of measure. Zoya divides debt and investments by market value. A screener that divides by total assets gets a different answer for the same company: Anthropic's debt is 47.9% of its total assets, and its interest-bearing investments are 91.6%. On that measure, the investments check fails as well. Two screeners can look at one company and reach different verdicts because they divide by different numbers.

The verdict can change. Interest income stays in the test for twelve months at a time, so Anthropic passes only when its revenue grows enough to pull the ratio under 5% and holds it there. Zoya calculates that quarterly revenue of about $23.7 billion, at the current level of interest, would bring a single quarter under the line.

For the investor in Ohio, the practical point is simple. The answer sits in the company's IPO filing, and the filing is public, so any investor can run the same three checks before buying.
