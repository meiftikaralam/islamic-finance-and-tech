### 🌍 1. IsDB and IFAD sign a $750 million deal to finance food and farming

The Islamic Development Bank and the UN's International Fund for Agricultural Development signed an agreement in Bangkok today to mobilize up to $750 million in joint financing for food systems and rural communities. The money will go toward farm supply chains, rural finance, water access, and climate adaptation across the countries both institutions serve. This continues a partnership that started in 1980. Together they have already co-financed 29 projects worth about $1.45 billion.

**What to learn:** The IsDB is a development bank owned by 57 Muslim-majority countries. It was set up in 1975 in Jeddah, and every project it finances follows Shariah. That rules out interest-based lending, so it uses leasing, installment sales, and equity stakes instead. Its work covers things ordinary people feel every day: roads, hospitals, schools, power lines, farms, and small business financing. In Mali it financed a bridge that replaced an unreliable ferry across the Niger River. Gambia is getting a new medical school to train doctors and nurses. Mauritania is getting an expanded cardiology center. In July the bank approved $297 million for projects across Côte d'Ivoire, Gambia, and Mauritania. The people it supports are mostly in its member countries across Africa, Asia, and the Middle East, plus Muslim communities elsewhere through scholarships and special funds.

For the USA, the picture is different. America is not a member, so the IsDB does not finance projects there. The connection runs through markets instead. In September the IsDB raised $1.5 billion selling sukuk, and 12 percent went to US-based investors, with Goldman Sachs among the banks arranging the sale. It has also partnered with the Gates Foundation in Seattle on health programs. So the USA touches the IsDB as an investor and partner, not as a borrower.

### 🇰🇪 2. Safaricom puts a Shariah-compliant investment fund inside M-Pesa

Safaricom and GulfCap Investment Bank launched the Ziidi Shari'ah Money Market Fund on October 6 for M-Pesa users in Kenya. Anyone can start with Ksh100, about 80 US cents, through the app or by dialing *334# on any phone. Top-ups start at Ksh10. Customers watch their profit grow daily and can withdraw back to M-Pesa for free. A lock feature lets people set money aside so they are not tempted to spend it.

**What to learn:** M-Pesa is a mobile money service. It started in Kenya in 2007 and lets people send money, pay bills, and save using a basic mobile phone, with no bank account needed. About 41 million people use it. A money market fund pools money from many small investors and puts it into very short-term, very safe instruments. The aim is to keep your capital safe while earning a small steady return, and you can take your money out any day. The Shariah version works the same way but only through Islamic banks and halal instruments, checked by GulfCap's Shariah board and regulated by Kenya's Capital Markets Authority. The regular Ziidi fund already holds about $145 million from over a million customers.

In the USA, this has not been adopted so far. No US service lets you put money into a Shariah money market fund from a phone menu the way M-Pesa does. Americans learning halal investing use apps like Wahed and Zoya, or halal ETFs like SPUS and HLAL. Those are built for stock investing through brokerages. The phone-first, start-with-a-dollar, Shariah money market product has no US equivalent yet.

### AI-powered Shariah financing for small businesses

A small medical equipment supplier in Riyadh has a chance to fulfill a big hospital order, but he needs stock first and the bank wants months of paperwork plus collateral he does not have. This is the problem AI-powered financing tries to solve.

It works like this. The business connects its real data: bank transactions, sales records, supplier payments. Software reads the cash flow and judges whether the business can repay. Approval takes about 48 hours instead of months. The financing itself follows Shariah rules, so there is no interest.

Here is what changes inside the process. A traditional bank does due diligence by hand. A loan officer collects months of statements, checks collateral, and waits on credit reports. That takes weeks, and small businesses often give up. With AI underwriting, the business connects its bank feed and sales records once. The software reads thousands of transactions in minutes: are customers paying on time, is revenue steady through the year, where does the cash actually go. It builds a risk picture from how money moves instead of from a stack of documents. And the watching does not stop at approval. The models keep tracking cash flow while the financing runs, so the lender sees trouble early instead of finding out at default.

Erad, a company in Riyadh, shows this in action. It raised $22 million in September to grow across the Gulf. Its models study a business's cash flow to set the financing size and keep watching risk as the relationship continues. It has lent over $133 million to small businesses so far, up to about $2.7 million per business, and its business in Saudi Arabia grew eight times in a year. Most of its customers come back for more financing within a year.

In the USA, no financial institution is doing business financing with AI underwriting. The closest are community lenders like Craft3 in Seattle, which offers Shariah-compliant business financing through partnership structures, and Blossom Finance, which uses technology to spread investments across halal micro-businesses. The US version of this product is still unbuilt.
