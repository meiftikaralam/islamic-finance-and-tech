### 🇬🇧 1. HSBC's Islamic fund faces new questions after $130.8 million found in five military-linked firms

A Middle East Eye analysis of HSBC's Islamic Global Equity Index Fund found it held about $130.8 million in shares of five companies linked to Israel's military during the first half of 2026. The five are Palantir ($38.3 million), RTX ($35.7 million), Analog Devices ($20.3 million), Honeywell ($18.9 million), and Rolls-Royce ($17.6 million), roughly 4 percent of the fund's $3.22 billion in assets. The fund sits inside most UK halal workplace pension schemes, so the holdings touch the retirement savings of large numbers of British Muslims. The Palantir purchase was first reported earlier this week; this analysis adds four more companies and puts a total figure on the exposure.

**What to learn:** The screening is done by the index, in two passes. HSBC's fund tracks the Dow Jones Islamic Market Titans 100 Index, so its managers buy whatever the index holds. The first pass checks what each company sells. The index removes companies in alcohol, tobacco, pork, conventional finance, entertainment, and weapons and defense. The tolerance rule decides who survives: a company is removed only when more than 5 percent of its revenue comes from those lines. A defense supplier stays in when its defense-linked sales sit under that 5 percent line. That is how the five companies in this story passed. The scholars behind the index treat defense as a revenue question for each company, not a supply-chain question about who buys the products.

The second pass checks the balance sheet. Three ratios must each stay under 33 percent of the company's average market value over the trailing 24 months: total debt, cash plus interest-bearing securities, and accounts receivable. Interest and other non-compliant income must stay under 5 percent of revenue. These tests read financial statements. They do not trace a chip from the factory to the weapons system it ends up in. The index rules are set by a Shariah supervisory board, and the index is reviewed each quarter, with changes taking effect in March, June, September, and December.

Once a company passes, the fund has no choice. HSBC's fund copies the index and holds its members in roughly index weights.

For an ordinary investor in the UK, the loose end is purification: donating the small share of non-compliant income that slips through the screen. [The Oct 12 edition explains how purification works.](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-12.html)

This is where it touches a real person. An ordinary investor in Birmingham who picked the halal option in her workplace pension owns a slice of these five companies without ever choosing a stock. Legal & General, Now: Pensions, and Scottish Widows all offer this fund as their Shariah option. The debate now has a voice in Parliament, where MP Afzal Khan called the holdings an affront to Islamic finance practice and urged the fund to sell. For readers, the practical lesson sits in today's Learn section: how to look inside your own fund and see what it holds.

### 🇮🇩 2. Indonesia to auction Rp10 trillion of sovereign sukuk on October 20

Indonesia's debt office announced it will auction eight series of sovereign sukuk on October 20, targeting Rp10 trillion (about US$600 million) to help fund the 2026 state budget. The sale mixes short-term Islamic treasury bills with longer-term sukuk. Both individuals and institutions can bid, but only through approved dealers.

**What to learn:** A short-term Islamic treasury bill and a long-term sukuk solve the same problem, borrowing without interest, but they work differently. The short bill is sold at a discount. The buyer pays less than face value today and receives the full amount at maturity. There are no payments in between, so there is no interest to pay. It suits money that is needed back soon.

The long-term sukuk is backed by real things: state assets or projects in the national budget. The government leases those assets back and the lease payments are the investor's return, paid over the life of the sukuk. It suits savings that can wait.

Both stay Shariah-compliant because the return comes from a real sale or a real lease, not from lending money at interest. An ordinary investor in Jakarta can bid for these through her bank, in the same auction where Bank Indonesia and the deposit insurer place their bids.

## Learn

### How to see what your halal fund actually owns

An ordinary investor picks the Shariah option in her workplace pension and assumes the screening does the rest. The HSBC story shows the label is only the start. Every fund publishes a factsheet, usually monthly, listing its largest holdings and the index it tracks. The semi-annual and annual reports go further: they list every purchase and sale. The Middle East Eye analysis was built on exactly that document. Those reports sit on the fund manager's website under "literature" or "documents."

Two more documents matter. The index methodology explains what the screen actually removes and what it keeps. The fund's prospectus names the Shariah board and the standards it follows. Reading those two tells you whether the screen covers only the standard business list or goes further.

None of this needs a finance degree. Download the factsheet, search the holdings for names you recognise, and compare the methodology against your own expectations. Check these once a year and you will know exactly where your money sits.

### Istisna — ordering something to be built

**What it is:** Istisna is a contract to make something that does not exist yet. You order a house, a machine, or a batch of goods to your specifications. The price, the specifications, and the delivery date are fixed in the contract. There is no interest and no loan.

**How it works:** Islamic law is normally strict about selling what does not exist. Istisna is the exception scholars carved out for manufacturing. The buyer can pay in full upfront, in stages as the work progresses, or on delivery. In parallel Istisna, the chain has three links: the customer orders from the bank, the bank orders from the builder, and the builder delivers. Each contract stands on its own, so if the builder fails, the bank still owes the customer the finished item.

**A real-world example:** Islamic banks use this to finance construction. A customer who owns a plot asks the bank to build a house. The bank signs a parallel Istisna with a builder, pays the builder as the work progresses, and hands the finished house to the customer, who pays the bank in instalments.
