# Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Wednesday, October 21, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

### 🇺🇸 1. Google starts checking finance advertisers in the US and Canada

Google announced on October 20 that its Financial Services Verification program will reach the United States and Canada in early 2027. Advertisers promoting certain financial services will have to prove they are authorized by a regulator or a recognized registry before their ads can run. Google says the program already covers 42 countries and about 2 billion internet users, and the United Kingdom has run it since 2021.

**What to learn:** This program is a credential check on top of Google's existing advertiser identity checks. It exists because scam finance ads steal real money. In the [Oct 10 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-10-10.html) (for more detail), a fake "Sharia-compliant" housing scheme in Minnesota defrauded Somali Muslim homebuyers. A family searching "halal mortgage" today can be led straight to a fraud.

The check works in three steps. Google asks the advertiser what financial service it provides, whether it is licensed to provide it, and for its registration number. Google then checks those answers against registries. In the United States it will use an American Bankers Association registry to confirm authorized banks, and in Canada it is working with the Canadian Bankers Association. An account that fails to complete verification in the time given gets paused.

The part that matters for Islamic finance is who sits in the ad results. US halal home finance providers — the small firms whose ads appear when a family searches — must now have their licence paperwork ready before Google notifies them. The rollout is phased, and Google will publish each country's accepted registries and credentials before enforcement starts. For the ordinary reader, the change means fewer scam ads standing between a search and a trustworthy provider.

### 🇬🇧 2. UK think tank puts a £450 million price on interest-free student loans

Equi, an Islamic think tank in the UK, released its "Have Faith in Higher Education" report on October 21, built on polling more than 1,300 students and 30 interviews. The report estimates that about 6,000 students a year skip university because student loans charge interest, and says enrolling them would add a £450 million economic premium per student cohort. It calls on the government to introduce interest-free alternative student finance for everyone.

**What to learn:** A UK student loan works like this. You borrow to cover tuition and living costs, and interest starts accruing from the day you receive the money. After graduation you repay 9% of your earnings above a set threshold, through the tax system. Any balance left after several decades is written off.

For some Muslim students, the interest is the barrier. Their faith prohibits riba, and no repayment structure changes the fact that interest accrues. The British Board of Scholars and Imams has likened the repayments to a graduate tax, but the report's numbers say about 6,000 students a year still choose no university over the current system.

The proposed fix uses takaful, the Islamic principle of mutual support. A takaful fund would be set up and run by the Student Loans Company, separate from the loan book. Students would apply through Student Finance England, receive the same tuition and living-cost payments, and after graduation pay contributions through the tax system back into the fund. Those contributions would be ringfenced for future students. Repayments would match loan repayments exactly, but no interest would accrue, and an independent Islamic finance board would certify the product.

The government has committed to introducing this product, but it is not available yet. The people waiting are students like the ones in Equi's interviews — young people who want the degree and refuse the interest, and who currently have no third option.

## Learn

### Three checks before you click a finance ad

Google's new verification will push scam ads off its results in the US and Canada, but checks you run yourself still matter. First, look the firm up on the regulator's public register — the FCA register in the UK, or your state's banking department in the US — and confirm the name matches the advertiser exactly. Second, check that a halal provider names its Shariah board, then check that those scholars are real people with real credentials. Third, read the ad's landing page and confirm it belongs to the licensed firm, not a lookalike name. Five minutes of checking protects money that no platform filter covers everywhere.

### A halal portfolio needs more than one kind of asset

Stocks can grow your money, and they can fall hard in the same year. Sukuk pay steady periodic income but grow little. Gold and cash tend to keep their value when markets fall. Holding more than one type means a bad year for one is rarely a bad year for everything. Spreading money across types of assets is what diversification means. Start with the mix, not with picking winners.

### Islamic windows vs full-fledged Islamic banks

**What it is:** A full-fledged Islamic bank does only Shariah-compliant business, from top to bottom. An Islamic window is a separate department inside a conventional bank that offers Shariah-compliant products while the rest of the bank runs conventionally.

**How it works:** The window keeps its money apart. It runs separate books and a separate pool of funds, so interest income from the conventional side does not mix with the window's money. A Shariah board oversees the window's products and audits them. The bank's staff and brand are shared, which is why the separation has to be documented and checked.

**A real-world example:** HSBC Amanah is the Islamic banking arm of HSBC, and Standard Chartered runs its Islamic business under the name Saadiq. A Muslim customer can walk into a conventional bank's branch and open an account that runs on the window's separate, Shariah-supervised books. A reader checking an ad should find out whether the provider is a full Islamic bank, a window inside a conventional bank, or an independent advisory. The answer changes who holds the money.
