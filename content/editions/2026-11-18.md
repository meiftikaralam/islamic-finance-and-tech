## Today's stories

### 🌍 1. A court blocks a winding-up petition against Zetrix AI

Zetrix AI has won a temporary court order stopping Macquarie Bank from serving a winding-up petition against the company. The dispute is over RM2.06 million (about $505,000) of dividends that Macquarie says it is owed under Zetrix's dividend reinvestment plan. High Court judge Wan Muhammad Amin Wan Yahya granted the order on November 17, and both sides will be heard on December 1.

**What to learn:** A winding-up petition is a creditor asking a court to close a company down. If the court agrees, it appoints a liquidator to sell the company's assets and pay its debts from the money raised. A petition is a serious step, because a company that is wound up stops trading and its shareholders usually lose everything.

Macquarie's claim starts with a statutory demand. Under section 466 of Malaysia's Companies Act 2016, a creditor can serve a formal demand for a debt it says is undisputed, and give the company 21 days to pay. If the company does not pay, the creditor can ask the court to wind it up. Macquarie served that demand on October 27, after Zetrix failed to pay a dividend it says was due on September 18.

The dividend comes from Zetrix's dividend reinvestment plan, dated August 26, 2026. Under the plan, a shareholder can take the dividend in cash or take new shares instead. Macquarie chose cash. At 2.89 sen a share on the 71,321,006 shares it says it owns, the cash came to RM2,061,177.07. Zetrix deferred the payment to September 28, and the money is still unpaid. Zetrix says Macquarie is not entitled to the payment, because the shares are registered in the name of Citigroup Nominees, which holds them for Macquarie, and the registered holder is the one the company recognises.

Zetrix went to court on November 10 and asked for an injunction, an order stopping Macquarie from serving the petition while the dispute is decided. The judge granted it on November 17 without Macquarie present, which is called an ex parte order. The order is temporary. Both sides will argue the case at a hearing on December 1, and the order does not decide whether Zetrix owes the money.

The people this touches are the sukukholders waiting on much larger sums. Zetrix has said it cannot repay the RM2.16 billion (about $529 million) outstanding under its sukuk programme, as covered in the [Nov 15 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-15.html). It still owes a RM25 million payment that was due on November 6, and Bursa Malaysia has told it to settle all outstanding cash dividends, RM49.76 million (about $12 million) in total, by November 23. Behind the sukukholders sit ordinary Malaysians whose savings are in the funds that hold the paper, including funds run by AmanahRaya Investment Management. A winding-up petition over RM2.06 million is small next to those sums, but it matters because a petition, if it succeeded, would put the company's future in a liquidator's hands. For now the court has paused that step, and the December 1 hearing is the next date to watch. Macquarie Bank is Australian, listed on the Australian Securities Exchange.

## Learn

### How a company is wound up

A winding-up is the legal process of closing a company down and selling everything it owns to pay what it owes. It ends with the company being removed from the register. The process is also called liquidation.

It starts when a creditor files a winding-up petition with the court. The creditor must show that the company owes it money and has failed to pay. The court holds a hearing, and if it agrees, it makes a winding-up order and appoints a liquidator. The liquidator is usually an accountant or a licensed insolvency practitioner. From that moment the directors lose control of the company, and the liquidator runs it.

The liquidator's job is to turn everything into cash. The liquidator sells the company's property, vehicles and equipment, collects money owed to the company, and then pays the creditors. The payments follow a fixed order set by law. The costs of the winding-up come first. Then come secured creditors, lenders with a claim on a named asset, such as a bank with a charge over a factory. Then come unsecured creditors, such as suppliers, landlords and workers owed wages. Shareholders come last, and they usually receive nothing.

Take a small example. A furniture maker owes its bank RM3 million, secured on its factory, and owes suppliers RM4 million. The liquidator sells the factory and everything else for RM5 million. The bank is paid its RM3 million first, because its loan is secured on the factory. RM2 million is left. The suppliers share that RM2 million between them, so each supplier receives 50 sen for every ringgit it is owed. The shareholders receive nothing, because the money ran out before their turn.

A court will not wind up a company over a debt the company genuinely disputes. The petition route is for debts that are clear and unpaid. That is the point Zetrix is arguing in its case against Macquarie: it says Macquarie is not entitled to the dividend, because the shares are registered to Citigroup Nominees. If the court accepts that the debt is genuinely disputed, the petition cannot go ahead on that debt.

For an ordinary investor, a winding-up is the last stage of a failure. The [Nov 7 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-07.html) explained what happens when a sukuk fails: the trustee calls a meeting and the holders vote on new terms. A winding-up is what happens when that negotiation fails and a creditor goes to court instead. Sukukholders rank as creditors for the money they are owed, behind any lender with security over a named asset, so the structure of the sukuk and the assets behind it decide how much they recover.
