### 🌍 1. Malaysia's first Shariah Bitcoin ETF plans a December 2 listing

Halogen Capital plans to list Malaysia's first Shariah-compliant spot Bitcoin ETF on Bursa Malaysia on December 2. The Securities Commission published the draft prospectus on November 19, and the public comment window runs to November 25. The fund would hold actual bitcoin and trade on the stock exchange like a share. Approval from the Securities Commission is still pending, and the fund cannot take money from investors until that approval comes. December 2 is the target listing date in the draft.

**What to learn:** Bitcoin is a hard fit for Islamic finance. Scholars are still debating whether bitcoin counts as money, as property, or as pure speculation. Because of that debate, Shariah-compliant bitcoin products are rare, and very few firms have tried to build one. Halogen runs a private Shariah bitcoin fund, started in August 2023, for a small group of investors. This ETF would put the same idea on a stock exchange, open to anyone.

The product works in a simple way. The fund collects money from investors and buys real bitcoin with it. At least 95% of the fund's money stays in bitcoin. The small remainder stays in Islamic money market instruments and Islamic deposits, ready for daily cash needs. Spot is the word for this kind of buying: the fund pays today's price and owns the bitcoin at once. The fund's value follows the FTSE Bitcoin Index. An index is a published number that tracks a price, and this one tracks the price of bitcoin, worked out over one hour each day up to 5pm Malaysia time. The fund aims to move up and down with that number, and it pays out no income.

Amanie Advisors is the fund's Shariah adviser. The fund can buy bitcoin only on exchanges registered with the Securities Commission, or on overseas platforms Amanie has approved. The cash part sits in Islamic instruments, so no part of the fund earns interest. Two firms hold the assets, and each has a separate job. BitGo, a company in the USA, keeps the bitcoin safe by holding the digital keys. Universal Trustee, a Malaysian company, holds the fund's assets for the investors and checks that Halogen follows the fund's rules.

The fees come out of the fund every year. Halogen charges up to 0.6% of the fund's value as its management fee. The trustee charges up to 0.05%, the index licence up to 0.04%, and the bitcoin custody up to 0.15%. Added together, the yearly cost can reach about 0.84%. On RM10,000 invested, that is up to about RM84 a year.

Now the customer's side. Take Ahmad in Kuala Lumpur, who already buys shares through a stockbroker. A stockbroker is the licensed company that runs his share account, the same account he uses to buy shares in a bank or an airline. If the listing goes ahead, Ahmad can buy this fund in that same account, in lots of 100 units, paying in ringgit or US dollars. He needs no crypto exchange account and no digital wallet of his own. Big orders move in a separate way: one dealer, CGS International, creates and cancels units in blocks of 50,000, delivering cash or bitcoin to the fund, and that block dealing keeps the unit price close to the value of the bitcoin behind it. Ahmad never deals with that part. He buys and sells the units that already trade on the exchange.

He puts money in by buying units at the market price, the way he buys a share. He takes money out by selling his units the same way, during exchange hours. His profit follows bitcoin. On RM10,000, a 20% rise in bitcoin takes his holding to about RM12,000 before fees. A 20% fall takes it to about RM8,000. The fund pays nothing along the way, so the price he sells at is his whole return. Halogen itself says the fund fits only investors with an extremely high-risk tolerance and a long time horizon. The steps from here are set out in the draft: comments close November 25, the formal prospectus comes on November 27, the first subscription is November 30, units are allotted December 1, and trading is planned to start on December 2.

### Guidance: how a home purchase plan works

A first-time buyer in Michigan wants a $400,000 house and has $80,000 saved. A conventional mortgage would lend her the other $320,000 and charge interest on it. A home purchase plan works differently. She and the finance provider buy the house together. She owns 20% and the provider owns 80%.

Her monthly payment has two parts. One part is rent for living in the provider's 80% share. The other part buys a slice of that share. Each month her share grows and the provider's share shrinks, so the rent part of her payment falls. After the full term she owns 100% of the house.

In the USA the largest provider is Guidance Residential. Its Declining Balance Co-ownership Program runs on diminishing musharaka, the partnership contract covered in the [Nov 13 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-13.html). Guidance has financed homes this way since 2002, and the programme is backed by Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae. Its Shariah board is chaired by Justice (Ret.) Muhammad Taqi Usmani. Guidance serves 37 states and Washington, D.C., and usually asks for a 20% down payment. Two other American providers use different contracts. UIF leases the home to the buyer under ijara, and Devon Bank buys the home and resells it at a fixed marked-up price under murabaha.

In the UK the main providers are Gatehouse Bank and AlRayan Bank. Gatehouse offers home purchase plans from a 5% deposit, with a two-year fixed rental rate of 5.68% in its October 29 product guide. The buyer still co-owns the home and buys the bank's share month by month. The structure is the same on both sides of the Atlantic. The buyer never borrows money at interest. She co-owns a house and buys out her partner.
