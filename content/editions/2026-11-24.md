### 🇺🇸 1. Walton Global closes the first Shariah-compliant facility in its US homebuilder land programme

Walton Global, a land investment firm based in Scottsdale, Arizona, has closed the first Shariah-compliant financing facility inside its Builder Land Finance programme. The programme finances land for large US homebuilders and has deployed more than $180 million so far, across more than 5,300 home sites in 23 projects in 8 states. The facility sits inside Walton's U.S. Land Income & Growth Fund, which Masryef Advisory has certified as Shariah-compliant. Walton does not state the size of the Shariah facility itself.

**What to learn:** A homebuilder needs land years before it sells the homes on it. Buying the land outright ties up the builder's cash, and borrowing the money for it means paying interest. Land banking is the middle route. A land banker buys the land the builder has chosen and holds it, and the builder takes the lots in phases as its homes sell.

Walton buys land its builder clients have picked for development in the near term. The land title stands as security for the financing. The builder then draws the land down phase by phase, so its land payments line up with its home sales instead of falling due all at once.

The standard version of this programme earns its return as interest on the financing. The new facility restructures that return so it comes from the land arrangement itself, with no interest charged, and Masryef Advisory has certified the structure. Islamic investors outside the USA gain access to income from US housing land in a form that follows the ban on interest, inside the US private credit market they could not reach before.

Walton runs the programme through its affiliate WLF 4.1, LLC. Tim Haywood, Walton's managing director for the Middle East, presents the fund to institutional and wealthy investors abroad. The fund is registered in the Cayman Islands for offshore investors, so US readers cannot buy it. The US door to private halal income is a product such as Cur8 Capital's USD Income Fund, covered in [today's post](https://islamicfintech.org/lab/cur8-usd-income-fund.html).

### Guidance: what a Muslim parent can do with a Trump Account

Sara, a mother in New Jersey, has a two-year-old daughter. Treasury enrolled eligible children automatically on October 1, 2026, so her daughter may already have a Trump Account. Sara can claim it to manage it. A Trump Account is a traditional IRA for a child, created by a 2025 law. A child qualifies with a valid Social Security number if she is still under 18 at the end of the year the account is opened.

Her daughter was born in 2025, so she qualifies for the seed. A US citizen born in 2025 through 2028 can elect a one-time $1,000 from the Treasury, on IRS Form 4547 or online. Family, friends and employers can add up to $5,000 a year in total, and the $1,000 seed sits on top of that limit. The money stays locked until December 31 of the year before her daughter turns 18. After that it moves to a regular traditional IRA in her daughter's name.

The problem sits in where the money goes. By default the $1,000 buys SPYM, a State Street fund that tracks the S&P 500. About $260 of every $1,000 sits in companies Zoya rates Non-Compliant, led by banks and Berkshire Hathaway. The five largest are Berkshire Hathaway at about $17, JPMorgan at about $14, Bank of America at about $5, Goldman Sachs at about $4 and Wells Fargo at about $4. The rest is spread across about 250 other companies. Zoya rates 258 of the fund's 503 companies Non-Compliant.

Treasury has named five funds for these accounts, and all five are conventional index funds that track their indexes with no Shariah screen: SPYM, IVV, VTI, SPTM and ITOT. Treasury expects to let parents split the money across the five funds later. The law also caps fees at 0.1% a year. The best-known Shariah-screened ETFs charge well above that: SPUS charges 0.45% and HLAL charges 0.50%, as the [Nov 22 edition](https://islamicfintech.org/editions/2026-11-22.html) shows. A future Shariah-screened option would need to meet the fee limit and the index rules, and whether one will exist is uncertain.

Scholars differ on what Sara should do. Darul Iftaa Chicago ruled in February 2026 that opening the account, accepting the $1,000 and adding contributions are all permissible, with the non-compliant share of dividend income estimated and given in charity. Darul Iftaa New York held off on a specific ruling in March 2026, treating the capital as halal and growth from non-compliant holdings as impermissible. Azzad Asset Management's Shariah board found no objection in August 2026 to accepting the $1,000 on its own, treating it as a gift, and advises holding off on family contributions until compliant options exist.

That leaves Sara with three paths, depending on the view she follows. She can claim the $1,000 and keep family money elsewhere, following Azzad. She can contribute and purify, following Darul Iftaa Chicago. Or she can wait for further clarification: the proposed rules would let her elect the $1,000 through December 31 of the year her daughter turns 17.

Whichever path she takes, two practical points follow. A custodial brokerage account lets her hold Shariah-compliant investments for her daughter outside the Trump Account. And when the restricted period ends, the money moves to a regular traditional IRA, where choosing Shariah-compliant funds becomes a separate step. Scholars also differ on zakat on a child's investments, so Sara asks a scholar how those rules apply to her daughter's account.
