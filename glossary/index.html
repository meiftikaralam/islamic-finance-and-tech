📖 Concepts

Sukuk
Islamic investment certificates. Instead of lending money and collecting interest (which is riba), sukuk holders own a share of a real asset or project — like an airport terminal — and earn returns from the profit it generates. Often called 'Islamic bonds,' but they are ownership certificates, not debt.
Sources: AAOIFI knowledge base: Sukuk
Islamic ETFs and Shariah stock screening
How an ETF holding Apple or Microsoft can be halal: the fund tracks an index of stocks screened against Islamic rules — no interest-based income, no alcohol, gambling or arms businesses, and strict limits on debt. A Shariah board certifies compliance, and any small leftover non-compliant income is 'purified' by donating it to charity.
Private credit
Loans made directly to companies by non-bank lenders (like investment funds) instead of through a bank. It has been one of the fastest-growing corners of conventional finance; Shariah-compliant versions structure the lending without interest — for example through cost-plus sales or profit-sharing deals.
Sources: Alternative Credit Investor: ASB StepStone fund
Fractional ownership
Owning a small slice of an expensive asset — like a fraction of a rental property — instead of buying the whole thing. Regulated exchanges now let everyday investors buy and sell these slices, which fits Islamic finance naturally since returns come from a real asset you partly own.
Sources: Alternative Credit Investor: GII / London House Exchange
Murabaha
A 'cost-plus' sale used across Islamic finance. The bank buys an asset (a house, car, or machine) and sells it to the customer at a disclosed price — its cost plus an agreed profit markup — paid in fixed installments. Because the bank genuinely buys and then sells the asset, this counts as trade rather than an interest-bearing loan. Example: halal home financing, where the bank buys a house and resells it to the buyer at a fixed marked-up price paid over years.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Learn section (Oct 8, 2026)
Additional Tier 1 (AT1) sukuk
A type of bank funding that sits between equity and ordinary debt: it can absorb losses if the bank gets into trouble, and the bank can usually redeem (call) it on a set date. Dubai Islamic Bank's $500 million AT1 sukuk, issued in 2021, is the Shariah-compliant version of this instrument — called on its first call date, October 19, 2026.
Sources: Reuters via TradingView — DIB to redeem $500m AT1 sukuk (Oct 7, 2026)
Short interest
The total number of a stock's (or ETF's) shares that investors have borrowed and sold, betting the price will fall. A rise in short interest means more traders are taking that side of the bet. Example: short interest in Wahed's HLAL halal ETF jumped 66% during September 2026 — though at under 1% of shares, it was still a small position.
Sources: Defense World — Wahed FTSE USA Shariah ETF short interest (Oct 2, 2026)
Sharia board
A panel of Islamic scholars that checks whether a bank's or fund's products and deals follow Islamic rules. Example: Al Marji Advisory was hired to set up and supervise an independent Sharia board for the Sentinel Shield Fund I.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — Sentinel Shield Fund I story
Special-purpose vehicle (SPV)
A separate legal company created to hold specific assets or investments, keeping them apart from the parent firm's other business. Example: the Sentinel Shield Fund I invests through SPVs focused on areas like unmanned systems and AI-enabled defense.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — Sentinel Shield Fund I story
Halal robo-advisor
An online service that builds and manages your investment portfolio automatically, using only Shariah-compliant investments. Example: Wahed, the largest halal robo-advisor for US investors, offers six risk levels from a $100 minimum.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — halal robo-advisors explainer
Islamic Financial Literacy Month
An annual October initiative by Saturna Capital that promotes Islamic financial education through in-person seminars and online webinars on topics such as halal retirement investing. Example: its October 14, 2026 webinar on keeping 401(k)s and IRAs halal.
Sources: Saturna Capital
401(k) and IRA
US retirement accounts where the government lets your savings grow with less tax as long as the money stays inside the account. For Muslim savers, the default investments inside many employer plans (bond or money-market funds) earn interest, so the picks often need switching to Shariah-screened funds.
Sources: Saturna Capital — Islamic Financial Literacy Month
