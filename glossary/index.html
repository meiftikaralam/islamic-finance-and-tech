📖 Concepts

Sukuk
Islamic investment certificates. Instead of lending money and collecting interest (which is riba), sukuk holders own a share of a real asset or project — like an airport terminal — and earn returns from the profit it generates. Often called 'Islamic bonds,' but they are ownership certificates, not debt.
Sources: AAOIFI knowledge base: Sukuk
Islamic ETFs and Shariah stock screening
How an ETF holding Apple or Microsoft can be halal: the fund tracks an index of stocks screened against Islamic rules — no interest-based income, no alcohol, gambling or arms businesses, and strict limits on debt. A Shariah board certifies compliance, and any small leftover non-compliant income is 'purified' by donating it to charity.
Private credit
Loans made directly to companies by non-bank lenders (like investment funds) instead of through a bank. It has been one of the fastest-growing corners of conventional finance; Shariah-compliant versions structure the lending without interest — for example through cost-plus sales or profit-sharing deals.
Sources: Alternative Credit Investor: ASB StepStone fund
Fractional ownership
Owning a small slice of an expensive asset — like a fraction of a rental property — instead of buying the whole thing. Regulated exchanges now let everyday investors buy and sell these slices, which fits Islamic finance naturally since returns come from a real asset you partly own.
Sources: Alternative Credit Investor: GII / London House Exchange
Murabaha
A 'cost-plus' sale used across Islamic finance. The bank buys an asset (a house, car, or machine) and sells it to the customer at a disclosed price — its cost plus an agreed profit markup — paid in fixed installments. Because the bank genuinely buys and then sells the asset, this counts as trade rather than an interest-bearing loan. Example: halal home financing, where the bank buys a house and resells it to the buyer at a fixed marked-up price paid over years.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Learn section (Oct 8, 2026)
Additional Tier 1 (AT1) sukuk
A type of bank funding that sits between equity and ordinary debt: it can absorb losses if the bank gets into trouble, and the bank can usually redeem (call) it on a set date. Dubai Islamic Bank's $500 million AT1 sukuk, issued in 2021, is the Shariah-compliant version of this instrument — called on its first call date, October 19, 2026.
Sources: Reuters via TradingView — DIB to redeem $500m AT1 sukuk (Oct 7, 2026)
Short interest
The total number of a stock's (or ETF's) shares that investors have borrowed and sold, betting the price will fall. A rise in short interest means more traders are taking that side of the bet. Example: short interest in Wahed's HLAL halal ETF jumped 66% during September 2026 — though at under 1% of shares, it was still a small position.
Sources: Defense World — Wahed FTSE USA Shariah ETF short interest (Oct 2, 2026)
Sharia board
A panel of Islamic scholars that checks whether a bank's or fund's products and deals follow Islamic rules. Example: Al Marji Advisory was hired to set up and supervise an independent Sharia board for the Sentinel Shield Fund I.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — Sentinel Shield Fund I story
Special-purpose vehicle (SPV)
A separate legal company created to hold specific assets or investments, keeping them apart from the parent firm's other business. Example: the Sentinel Shield Fund I invests through SPVs focused on areas like unmanned systems and AI-enabled defense.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — Sentinel Shield Fund I story
Halal robo-advisor
An online service that builds and manages your investment portfolio automatically, using only Shariah-compliant investments. Example: Wahed, the largest halal robo-advisor for US investors, offers six risk levels from a $100 minimum.
Sources: Oct 9 edition — halal robo-advisors explainer
Islamic Financial Literacy Month
An annual October initiative by Saturna Capital that promotes Islamic financial education through in-person seminars and online webinars on topics such as halal retirement investing. Example: its October 14, 2026 webinar on keeping 401(k)s and IRAs halal.
Sources: Saturna Capital
401(k) and IRA
US retirement accounts where the government lets your savings grow with less tax as long as the money stays inside the account. For Muslim savers, the default investments inside many employer plans (bond or money-market funds) earn interest, so the picks often need switching to Shariah-screened funds.
Sources: Saturna Capital — Islamic Financial Literacy Month
Ijara wa Iqtina
A lease-to-own contract used in Islamic finance. The financier buys the asset and stays its legal owner while the customer pays fixed rent; ownership transfers at the end of the term. Because the payment is rent rather than loan repayment, no interest is involved. Example: Ayan Capital's halal car finance in the UK.
Sources: PR Newswire — Ayan Capital secures $100M facility (Sep 2026)
Accredited investor
In the US, an investor the SEC lets into private investments that are not open to the public: income above $200,000 a year ($300,000 with a spouse), net worth above $1 million excluding the home, or certain finance licences.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Oct 11, 2026
Zakat on investments
Zakat is the yearly obligation to give 2.5% of qualifying wealth to charity, and investments count as wealth. For stocks and funds held as a long-term investment, most scholars say you pay on the market value on your zakat due date; traders pay on the full market value of holdings treated as trade goods. Retirement accounts such as a 401(k) also count, though some scholars allow delaying payment until withdrawal.
Sources: Amal Invest — how much zakat to pay on stocks
Purification
Purification means giving away the small haram income inside an otherwise halal investment, instead of keeping it. Even screened companies and funds can earn a little interest on cash balances, and Shariah standards tolerate up to 5% of revenue from such sources. Investors donate that impure portion of their dividends to charity. It is separate from zakat: zakat is the obligation on wealth, purification cleans income you should not have kept.
Sources: HalalWallet — why tech stocks land in 'questionable' and how investors handle purification
Riba
Riba means interest: any guaranteed extra charged for the use of money. It is prohibited in the Quran and is the main dividing line between Islamic and conventional finance. Islamic finance replaces interest with shared risk — the financier shares in profit, charges rent for an asset, or sells goods at a price paid over time — so returns come from real economic activity.
Sources: Investopedia — What is Riba?
Mudaraba
A partnership where one side provides the capital (rabb al-mal) and the other provides the work and skill (mudarib). Profits are split by an agreed percentage; losses fall on the capital provider, while the manager loses their effort — unless they were negligent. Islamic banks use it for savings accounts, with depositors as capital providers and the bank as manager.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 13 2026
52-week high
The highest price a stock or fund has traded at over the past year. When the halal ETF SPUS reached $60.49 in October 2026, it marked a new 52-week high — meaning buyers were paying more than at any point in the previous twelve months.
Sources: The Cerbat Gem: SPUS sets new 1-year high
Expense ratio
The yearly fee for owning a fund, shown as a percentage of your investment — for example, a 0.50% expense ratio costs $50 a year on $10,000. It is taken from the fund's assets, so you never see a separate bill. Halal funds often charge more than broad market funds because screening and audits add costs.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 14 2026
Sukuk redemption (call)
When a sukuk issuer buys back its certificates on a call date named in the original terms, paying holders full face value plus any profit payments still owed. The certificates then stop trading and the listing is cancelled. A redemption is a planned contract step, different from a default, where the issuer fails to pay.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 14 2026
Money market fund
A fund that pools money from many small investors and puts it into very short-term, very safe instruments. The aim is to keep your capital safe while earning a small steady return, and you can withdraw any day. A Shariah version only uses halal instruments.
Sources: Hivileo
Ijara
An Islamic lease contract: one party owns an asset and the other pays fixed monthly rent to use it, with no interest. In the lease-to-own form (ijara wa iqtina), each payment is split between rent and a contribution toward ownership, so the user ends up owning the asset at the end of the term.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Oct 16, 2026 edition (Ijara explainer)
Sukuk programme
A pre-approved framework letting an issuer sell sukuk in multiple tranches up to a set ceiling, instead of arranging each sale from scratch. Example: OQEP's $2B programme lets the Omani energy company issue tranches whenever market conditions suit.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 16 2026
Municipal sukuk
Sukuk issued by a city or municipal government to fund public services or refinance existing debt. Example: Greater Amman Municipality's JD400M ijara sukuk — Jordan's first — to replace expensive conventional loans.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 16 2026
Tokenised sukuk
A sukuk whose ownership is recorded as digital tokens on a shared ledger (blockchain) instead of a paper registry. The Shariah structure underneath is unchanged; tokenisation changes the record-keeping and can cut the investment into small pieces so ordinary people can buy in. Example: Malaysia's planned retail tokenised government sukuk under Budget 2027.
Sources: LEMON Blog — Malaysia plans tokenised government sukuk for retail investors (Oct 2026) · Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 17 2026
Istisna
A contract to manufacture or build something that does not exist yet, to the buyer's specifications, with the price and delivery date fixed in advance. There is no interest and no loan. Example: an Islamic bank commissions a contractor to build a house on a customer's plot, pays the builder as work progresses, and the customer pays the bank in instalments.
Sources: Islamic Finance News glossary — Istisna · Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 17 2026
Actively managed ETF
An exchange-traded fund where professionals pick the stocks instead of copying an index. The Almalia Shariah ETF on the London Stock Exchange is the first actively managed global Shariah equity ETF.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 18 2026
Sukuk covenant
A promise written into a sukuk’s terms, such as keeping enough cash on hand. Breaking it is a breach that can lead to a waiver vote or a dissolution event.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 18 2026
Dissolution event (sukuk)
The sukuk equivalent of a default. If triggered, the trustee can accelerate: every outstanding sum becomes due immediately. Example: Zetrix AI’s Sukuk Wakalah, with RM2.0 billion in principal at risk.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 18 2026
Waqf
A permanent charitable endowment in Islamic law. The assets are locked in forever and only their income is spent, for example to fund mosques, schools, or scholars.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 18 2026
Salam
A contract for buying goods in advance: the buyer pays the full price now for goods delivered later, with goods, quantity, price, and delivery date fixed in the contract. Example: an Islamic bank paying a wheat farmer at planting time for delivery after harvest.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 18 2026
Wakala
An agency contract: an investor appoints an agent (wakil) to invest money under an agreed set of rules, for a fixed fee. Profits belong to the investor. Example: SkyWorld's Sukuk Wakalah, where investors appoint SkyWorld Capital as their agent to fund property development.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 19 2026
Panda bonds
Bonds sold in China by a foreign borrower, denominated in yuan. They let a country borrow from Chinese investors instead of only dollar markets. Example: Pakistan's RMB 7.2 billion Panda bond programme, first issued in May 2026.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 19 2026
Islamic REIT
A real estate investment trust that follows Shariah rules: it owns income-generating property, avoids interest-bearing debt, and keeps tenants in permissible businesses. Investors earn from rental income. Example: Al Mal Capital REIT on the Dubai Financial Market, which finances its properties with Corporate Ijarah facilities.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 19 2026
Tender offer
When a company offers to buy back its own bonds or sukuk certificates from investors for cash, usually to refinance on better terms. Example: Arada's October 2026 offer to buy back $411.7 million of its 2027 sukuk at face value.
Sources: Investegate RNS
Financial Services Verification
Google's program that requires financial-services advertisers to prove they are authorized by a regulator or a recognized registry before their ads can run. It started in the UK in 2021 and reaches the US and Canada in early 2027. Example: a halal mortgage provider advertising on Google will need to show its licence.
Sources: Search Engine Land
Alternative student finance
The UK's planned interest-free student finance product, built on the Islamic principle of takaful: students receive tuition and living-cost payments from a mutual fund and later pay contributions into it, ringfenced for future students, with no interest.
Sources: GOV.UK
Takaful (mutual support)
The Islamic principle of mutual support: a group pools contributions into a shared fund that pays out when members need it. The UK's planned alternative student finance uses a takaful fund — graduates' contributions pay for future students, with no interest involved.
Sources: GOV.UK
Tokenized bond
A bond recorded as a digital token on a shared ledger instead of in a traditional securities depository. Ownership changes when the token moves, and payment can settle at the same time. A tokenized sukuk still needs the same asset-backed structure and Shariah approvals as a conventional one — only the record-keeping changes.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 22 2026
Islamic core banking platform
Banking software built around Islamic contracts from the start. Conventional systems assume loans with interest; an Islamic core records who owns the asset at each step, rental schedules, and profit-sharing ratios. Example: Nigeria's Peerless built MIZAN, and alt bank moved all its services onto it in October 2026.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 22 2026
Islamic credit card
A credit card that follows Shariah principles: the bank charges no interest. Instead it earns a profit rate on balances the customer carries over. Some, like Bank Muamalat's 2026 Awwal Card-i, use non-compounding profit — profit is charged only on what the customer owes, never on profit added in earlier months.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 22 2026
Syndicated financing
A single large financing shared by several banks. One bank arranges the deal and sets the terms, and other banks join, each taking a share of the facility, so the borrower deals with one agreement instead of negotiating with each bank separately. Example: Digital Nasional Berhad's MYR5.2 billion facility, shared by Maybank Investment Bank, AmInvestment, CIMB Islamic, RHB Islamic, and UOB Malaysia.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 24 2026
Regulatory sandbox
A supervised trial space where a regulator lets a new financial service run live with real clients under limits, such as caps on client numbers and transaction volumes, while it checks the service before granting a full licence. Example: Oman's Financial Services Authority trial of the Rushd automated advice platform.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 24 2026
Payment processing
The system that moves a card payment from a shop to the customer's bank. The processor checks the transaction and routes it to the bank, and the back-office work that follows matches payments to accounts, settles the money, and keeps the records straight. Example: Network International processing card payments for Emirates Islamic.
Sources: Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 24 2026
Covered card
The Shariah-compliant version of a credit card. The bank charges set fees for the card instead of interest on unpaid balances, and earns from the annual fee and the commission merchants pay when the card is used. Example: the ADIB Aldar Visa cards launched in October 2026.
Sources: ADIB · Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 25 2026
Collateralised murabaha
A murabaha sale backed by pledged assets as security. The bank buys an asset and sells it at a disclosed markup paid in instalments, and the pledged collateral gives the lender something to fall back on if the borrower cannot pay. Example: NBB Group's sustainable murabaha in Bahrain.
Sources: BizBahrain · Islamic Finance Daily Brief, Oct 25 2026
Supply chain finance
Financing that pays a supplier early for goods already shipped, instead of making the supplier wait until the buyer's payment date. The financier pays the supplier a slightly discounted amount now and collects the full amount from the buyer later. A Shariah-compliant version earns its return through a sale or lease structure, not interest.
Sources: Trade Treasury Payments — ITFC puts Islamic finance behind open account trade (Oct 26, 2026)
Tawarruq
A chain of sales used to raise cash without interest. A person buys a commodity on deferred payment, then sells it on for cash and keeps the cash. Each sale is a separate contract, and the cash comes from a real sale of a real commodity.
Sources: Trade Treasury Payments — ITFC puts Islamic finance behind open account trade (Oct 26, 2026)
Factoring
Selling unpaid invoices to a company, called a factor, for cash now. The factor pays most of the invoice value within a day or two and collects the full amount from the customer later. For example, a supplier with a $10,000 invoice on 60-day terms might receive $8,500 now and the rest, minus a fee, when the customer pays.
Sources: Merchant Maverick — Invoice factoring guide · Oct 26 edition
Islamic window
A section inside a conventional bank that offers Shariah-compliant services, with its own products, records, and Shariah oversight, while the rest of the bank continues its conventional business. For example, Uzbekistan's Open Bank and Bangladesh's Community Islamic both operate as Islamic windows inside conventional banks.
Sources: Spot — Uzbekistan issues first licence for Islamic banking · Oct 27 edition
Qard Hasan
A benevolent loan in Islamic finance. The borrower repays exactly the amount borrowed, with no interest and no extra charge, even if repayment runs late. For example, a community fund lends a shopkeeper money to restock, and she repays it in instalments from her sales.
Sources: Oct 28 edition
Yield
The return earned on money over a year, shown as a percentage. For example, lending $100 and getting $105 back after one year is a 5% yield.
Sources: Oct 28 edition
Discounting
Working out what money promised in the future is worth today. For example, at 5% a year, $1,000 due in three years is worth $863.84 today.
Sources: Oct 28 edition
Segregated portfolio
One legal fund company divided into separate compartments. Each compartment holds its own assets, so the assets of one portfolio are kept apart from the others. For example, Perennial sits as one compartment inside the Skye Investment Fund, with its own share classes and fees.
Sources: Oct 29 edition
Supplementary Retirement Scheme (SRS)
Singapore's voluntary retirement savings scheme. A saver puts money aside for retirement and gets tax relief on contributions. For example, a Singapore saver can use SRS money to buy the Singapore-dollar Islamic class of a physical gold fund.
Sources: Oct 29 edition
Assets under management (AUM)
The total value of client money a firm looks after. For example, SP Wealth Group reported more than $5 billion in assets under management in October 2026, up from $3 billion in December 2025.
Sources: Oct 29 edition
Murabaha Share Financing (MSF)
A way to finance a share purchase without an interest-bearing loan. The financier buys shares and sells them to the investor at a disclosed markup, paid over time. Pakistan's Shariah trading counter offers it as the alternative to conventional margin lending.
Sources: Oct 30 edition
Treasury sukuk (T-Sukuk)
A short- to medium-term sukuk issued by a government to raise money in its own currency. Example: the UAE government sold a T-Sukuk tranche maturing in October 2029 in its October 2026 auction.
Sources: Mid-East.info, Oct 31 2026
Initial public offering (IPO)
The first sale of a company's shares to the public on a stock exchange. A company issues new shares, investors buy them, and the shares can then be traded on the exchange. AdalFi's planned Rs652 million IPO would make it the first fintech listed on the Pakistan Stock Exchange.
Sources: FWDstart — AdalFi IPO plan (Oct 30, 2026)
Savings Sukuk
A government sukuk sold directly to ordinary citizens in small amounts, starting from IDR 1 million in Indonesia. It cannot be traded or transferred to another investor, but the holder can withdraw up to 50% early, and its return moves with the central bank rate subject to a guaranteed minimum.
Sources: Aktualita — SBN ST017 opens November 6, 2026
Rights Issue
A way for a listed company to raise cash by offering new shares to its existing shareholders first, usually below the market price and in proportion to what they already hold. A shareholder who does not buy sees their percentage ownership fall, which is called dilution.
Sources: Mettis Global — Zarea plans Rs3bn rights issue (Nov 2, 2026)
Trade Finance
Financing that covers the gap between goods being shipped and payment arriving, often 60 to 90 days later. The financier pays the exporter early or guarantees payment, tied to a specific trade and its documents, and is repaid when the buyer pays.
Sources: TNGlobal — mamun raises $3M seed (Nov 4, 2026)
Project finance
Borrowing to build or buy one specific project, where the loan is repaid from the money that project earns. A common example is a government concession: the government pays a company fixed amounts to run a service, and those payments repay the financing.
Sources: MARC Ratings — preliminary AA+IS rating for Weststar Aviation sukuk (Nov 4, 2026)
Parallel Istisna
Two Istisna contracts back to back. In the first, a financier agrees to deliver a finished building to investors; in the second, the financier hires a builder to construct it. Money from the sukuk pays the builder in stages, and the finished building satisfies the first contract. Example: Medtech Maldives' MVR 80 million sukuk to build a healthcare complex in Malé.
Sources: Maaldif — Medtech Maldives seeks MVR 80 million sukuk (Nov 6, 2026)
Shariah audit
The check that a bank's transactions followed the rulings of its Shariah board. An internal team reviews transactions after the event, and an external audit reviews that work independently. When a transaction fails the check, the income from it cannot be kept as profit.
Sources: The24X7News — SITR opens Bahrain operations (Nov 4, 2026)
Musawamah
A sale where the buyer and seller agree a price without the seller disclosing what the item cost. The price is fixed in advance and paid in instalments or in full. Example: PalmPay Pakistan's Shariah-compliant smartphone financing.
Sources: PalmPay Pakistan — official website
Technical default
A breach of a rule in a sukuk or bond agreement while payments are still being made on time. It is different from a missed payment: the issuer broke a term, such as holding too little in a reserve account, rather than failing to pay. It usually comes with a remedy period to fix the breach. Example: YNH Property's reserve account shortfall in November 2026.
Sources: KLSE Screener — YNH Property sukuk reserve shortfall (Nov 6, 2026)
Reserve account (sukuk)
A pot of money a sukuk issuer must build up before a maturity date, so the cash is already there when the repayment falls due. The agreement sets the amounts and dates. If the balance falls short, the issuer is in technical default and usually has a remedy period to top it up. Example: YNH Property had to hold RM61.2 million by October 27, 2026, ahead of a RM170 million maturity in February 2027.
Sources: KLSE Screener — YNH Property sukuk reserve shortfall (Nov 6, 2026)
Perpetual sukuk
A sukuk with no maturity date. The issuer pays profit at set intervals and never has to repay the principal on a fixed date, though it can usually redeem the sukuk on a call date if it chooses. It ranks below ordinary debt, so holders are paid after senior creditors if the issuer runs into trouble, and the profit rate is usually higher to reflect that extra risk. Example: Ominvest's OMR 100 million sukuk from November 2026 pays 6.50% a year and has no fixed maturity.
Sources: Dealroom — Ominvest raises $260M in oversubscribed perpetual sukuk (Nov 7, 2026)
Core banking system
The software that keeps a bank's central records: every account, balance, payment, loan and deposit. Staff and the bank's app both read from the same records. Example: in November 2026 AlRayan Bank agreed to move its core banking onto Monument Technology's cloud platform.
Sources: The Intermediary — AlRayan Bank announces largest-ever technology investment in Monument partnership (Nov 9, 2026)
French lease
A tax-driven way to pay for an aircraft under French law. A leasing company owned by French taxpayers buys the plane and writes its value down quickly, which cuts the investors' tax bills, and part of that saving reaches the airline as lower rent. The airline pays rent to use the plane and does not own it during the lease. Example: in November 2026 Emirates funded two Airbus A350-900 aircraft through a 12-year Shariah-compliant French lease.
Sources: Reed Smith — French tax leases for aircraft, advantages and limitations · Emirates — Emirates secures innovative Shariah-compliant French lease financing for two Airbus A350-900 aircraft (Nov 9, 2026)
Banking Platform as a Service (BPaaS)
A way for a bank to rent its core banking software instead of building and running its own. The provider hosts the software in the cloud and handles updates, and the bank pays a fee. Example: Monument Technology's Bank in a Box platform, which AlRayan Bank chose in November 2026.
Sources: The Intermediary — AlRayan Bank announces largest-ever technology investment in Monument partnership (Nov 9, 2026) · Monument Technology — About Us
Stablecoin
A digital token designed to hold a steady value, usually one token equal to one US dollar. The issuer holds a dollar or short-term US government debt in reserve for each token, so a person can buy one token for one dollar and redeem it for a dollar. Example: an investor can receive a sukuk profit payment in a stablecoin in minutes, while a bank transfer across borders can take days.

Smart contract
A piece of software on a blockchain that carries out an agreed action automatically when its conditions are met, with no person typing the instruction in. Example: on WafrChain, smart contracts route sukuk profit payments automatically when the payment date in the schedule arrives.

Permissioned blockchain
A blockchain that only approved participants can join, unlike a public blockchain anyone can use. Each participant sees only its own activity, so positions stay private from one another. Example: WafrChain is a private permissioned blockchain for sukuk, open to approved issuers, distributors and investors.
Sources: GlobeNewswire — Wafr Announces WafrChain for On-Chain Shariah-Compliant Sukuk and Pre-Seed Funding (Nov 10, 2026)
Alternative Investment Fund (AIF)
A pooled fund in India, regulated by the market regulator SEBI, that collects money from wealthy individuals and institutions to invest in private assets such as real estate and unlisted companies. Category II is the category for such funds. Example: HiLITE Ethical Fund 1, a Shariah-certified real estate AIF launched in November 2026.
Sources: The Times of Bengal — HiLITE Group to Launch Rs. 200 Crore Shariah-Certified Category II AIF (Nov 11, 2026)
Retail sukuk
A sukuk sold in small amounts directly to ordinary savers, rather than in large lots to banks and funds. Indonesia has sold retail sukuk from Rp1 million since 2009, and one series in 2026 drew 93,548 investors. A savings sukuk is a retail sukuk that cannot be traded onward.
Sources: FinFormed — Why Do So Few Ordinary Muslims Own Sukuk? (Nov 11, 2026)
