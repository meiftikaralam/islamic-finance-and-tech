Sukuk — A Beginner's Guide

Knowledge base · Topic 1

In one sentence

Sukuk are certificates that give you ownership in a real asset (like a building or a highway), and you earn money from what that asset produces — not from interest.

Why does sukuk exist?

In Islamic finance, charging or paying interest (riba) is not allowed. But governments and companies still need to raise large amounts of money, and investors still want a return. Sukuk is the answer: a way to raise money and earn returns while following Islamic rules.

How it works — step by step

  1. A government or company needs money — say, $1 billion to build an airport.
  2. Instead of borrowing at interest, it creates sukuk certificates linked to the airport.
  3. Investors buy the certificates. They now collectively own a share of the airport.
  4. As the airport earns money (landing fees, shops, parking), investors receive their share of the profits.
  5. At the end of the agreed period, the government buys the airport share back, returning the investors' original money.

No interest is charged at any point. Returns come from a real, productive asset.

The main types of sukuk

  • Ijara sukuk — backed by a leased asset; investors earn rent from it. The most common type.
  • Wakala sukuk — investors appoint an agent to invest their money in halal activities; they share the returns.
  • Mudaraba sukuk — a partnership where investors provide the money and a manager runs the project; profits are shared.
  • Musharaka sukuk — a joint venture; everyone invests together and shares profit and loss.
  • Murabaha sukuk — linked to a cost-plus sale; less common for trading.

What AAOIFI says

AAOIFI (the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions) is the global body that writes the rulebook for Islamic finance. Its Shariah Standard No. 17 — Investment Sukuk defines sukuk as certificates of equal value representing undivided shares in ownership of tangible assets, usufruct and services, or the assets of particular projects.

In plain words: each sukuk must represent a real slice of real ownership — not just a promise to pay.

In February 2008, AAOIFI issued an important clarification: for sukuk to be tradable (bought and sold on markets), holders must truly own the underlying asset, with all the rights and responsibilities of ownership. Paper promises without real assets behind them don't qualify.

Key terms — simple glossary

Riba
Interest; any guaranteed extra charged on a loan. Not allowed in Islamic finance.
Halal
Permissible under Islamic rules.
Usufruct
The right to use something and benefit from it (for example, living in a building you partly own).
Originator
The government or company raising money through sukuk.
SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle)
A separate company set up just to hold the asset on behalf of sukuk holders.
Shariah board
Scholars who check that a sukuk follows Islamic rules.
