Guidance: deposit accounts vs investment accounts

A woman in Michigan opens an Islamic savings account online. She puts in $5,000. The first thing to know is what the bank does with that money, because Islamic banks offer two kinds of account and they work in opposite ways.

A deposit account is the bank holding your money for you. The contract is usually qard, a loan from you to the bank. The bank owes you the full amount back whenever you ask for it. It pays no return on the money. A current account works this way. Your money is safe, and the bank guarantees it back.

An investment account is you investing with the bank. The contract is usually mudaraba, a profit-sharing partnership, or wakala, where the bank acts as your agent. Your money joins a pool with other depositors' money. The bank invests the pool in Shariah-compliant financing: home purchases, car sales, leases, sukuk. The pool's profit is split between the bank and the depositors at a ratio agreed in advance. The return is not guaranteed. If the pool earns less, you get less. If the pool loses money, the loss falls on the depositors, unless the bank was negligent.

The difference shows up in the numbers. Say the pool earns enough to pay a 4% return one year. A depositor with $10,000 in an investment account gets about $400. The same $10,000 in a deposit account gets nothing, and the bank still owes the full $10,000 back. The investment account pays more because the depositor takes the risk. The deposit account pays nothing because the bank takes the risk.

The UAE's new rulebook, covered in the Nov 13 edition, lists Islamic deposits and investment accounts as two separate categories, with separate rules for each. The distinction matters in the USA too. University Bank's UIF arm runs its accounts on wakala, with the bank as agent. Stearns Bank's Salaam Banking and Bank of Whittier offer Islamic accounts nationwide, and all three banks are FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor. Before opening an account, check which kind it is: a deposit the bank owes back, or an investment that shares the pool's profit and loss.

Guidance: how an Islamic bank sets the return on your savings

An ordinary investor in Dubai sees an advert for an Islamic savings account: 5.25% expected. The word to notice is "expected". A conventional bank promises its interest rate in advance. An Islamic bank cannot promise a return on money, because a guaranteed return on money is interest. So it publishes a target based on what its pool of financing actually earned.

The money in the account joins a pool. The bank uses the pool to fund Shariah-compliant financing: home leases, car sales, trade deals, sukuk. At the end of each period the bank works out what the pool earned. That profit is split between the bank and the depositors at a ratio agreed in advance. Meezan Bank in Pakistan publishes a 50/50 split on its savings pool: half the profit to the bank, half to depositors. BankIslami publishes 50/50 on its general savings pool and 70/30 in the customer's favour on term deposits, because money locked away longer earns a bigger share.

The split is only the first step. Each kind of account gets a weight, based on its size and term, so a long-term deposit earns more per dollar than a basic savings account from the same pool. Banks also hold back some profit in good months into a reserve, called a profit equalisation reserve, and release it in weak months to keep the rate steady. A second reserve absorbs losses before they reach depositors.

The rate also moves with the wider economy. The pool earns from financing whose returns track the general level of rates, so when rates in the economy rise, the pool earns more and the declared rate rises with it. Albaraka Türk, Türkiye's oldest participation bank, redeemed TRY 2.25 billion (about $45 million at about 50 lira to the dollar) of lease certificates on November 13, and its returns are set with reference to the central bank's policy rate, which stood at 37% through September, and an inflation forecast of 29.5% for the year end. The bank's board approved a programme of up to TRY 80 billion (about $1.6 billion) of new certificates in May 2026. The return looks like an interest rate, and it moves like one, because the financing it comes from is priced against the same market.

The check for an ordinary investor is the published record. Banks publish their declared rates and their profit-sharing ratios each month. The rate sheet shows the split, the weights, and the reserves. A rate labelled "expected" is a target from past pool earnings, not a promise. The Nov 8 edition covered halal savings accounts in the USA and how the bank earns the profit behind them. The return on an Islamic savings account is a share of what real financing earned, worked out after the fact, not a price fixed in advance.
