Guidance: is a health savings account halal?

Amina in Ohio has a health savings account through her job. Her employer puts money in before tax, the balance grows without tax, and she can spend it on medical bills without tax. The account is useful, and the question is whether the way it works fits Islamic rules.

Two published rulings address the account directly. The Assembly of Muslim Jurists of America ruled in 2021 that having an HSA is permissible, with a condition on interest: stay away from interest earnings where you can, or give away any that reach your investments. Darul Iftaa Chicago ruled in 2024 that contributing to an HSA and investing through it is permissible, with two conditions: invest in Shariah-compliant funds, and give any interest earned to charity.

The cash is where the question starts. Most large HSA providers put uninvested cash in interest-bearing options by default. At Fidelity, uninvested cash goes into a government money market fund or a bank sweep, and both pay interest. Fidelity lets you invest from the first dollar, so money you invest stops sitting in interest-bearing cash.

Saturna's Shifa HSA takes a different route. Saturna says the spending account tied to its debit card is non-interest-bearing, and the rest goes into Amana mutual funds. You keep $500 in the spending account before you invest. HSA Bank's Choice investment list includes SPUS, HLAL and SPSK, a fund of sukuk. SPUS and HLAL were compared in the Nov 22 edition.

A worked example shows how the pieces fit. Say a family has HSA-eligible cover for all of 2026 and opens an HSA at Fidelity. The family and the employer put in $8,750 combined, the 2026 family limit, since employer contributions count toward the limit. The family plans to pay this year's medical costs from other savings, so the $8,750 is long-term money. It can go into a Shariah-screened ETF such as SPUS, if the account offers it. New contributions, dividends and sale proceeds land in cash first, so the family checks the cash balance regularly and invests what is meant for the long term. A small amount of interest may be credited before the money is invested, and under the Chicago ruling that interest goes to charity, paid from any of the family's wealth.

Zakat applies to the account each year. Shaykh Joe Bradford holds that zakat is due because the money is yours from the day it goes in, it rolls over, and you can withdraw it penalty free for qualified medical costs at any time. Cash counts at face value, and funds count by the current assets per share of the companies underneath.

One more question sits behind the account itself. An HSA needs an HSA-eligible health plan, and health insurance raises its own question. The International Islamic Fiqh Academy prohibits commercial insurance and permits takaful, cooperative insurance. AMJA allows health insurance only as needed in North America, because of the very high cost of healthcare. A reader with an HSA should ask a qualified scholar how this applies to them.

Starting in 2026, qualifying bronze and catastrophic plans also count as HSA-eligible, even ones bought outside a health insurance marketplace. And if you change jobs, the account stays yours. IRS rules allow direct transfers from one HSA trustee to another, as many times as you like.

Guidance: how halal car financing works in the USA

Yusuf in Michigan needs a car for work. A conventional auto loan charges interest on the money borrowed, so he looks for a way to pay for the car without an interest loan. In the USA, the main route is a sale.

The provider buys the car first, then sells it to the buyer at a higher agreed price, paid in monthly instalments. The extra amount is a disclosed profit on a sale. The price is fixed at signing and does not grow if a payment is late. The provider must own the car before reselling it, even briefly, because that ownership is what turns the margin into trading profit instead of interest. Most US programmes follow this murabaha logic, which the Oct 8 edition explains.

UIF Corporation is the main dedicated provider. It merged with LARIBA on April 1, 2026, and kept vehicle financing in-house under its independent Sharia Advisory Board. UIF signs a Joint Purchase Agreement with the buyer once a price is agreed with the seller, then a Motor Vehicle Installment Sale Contract transfers ownership to the buyer, who pays UIF monthly.

The programme is small and limited by state. UIF finances vehicles only for residents of Texas, Michigan, Ohio and Illinois, and the car must be registered in one of those states. A buyer elsewhere has no equivalent nationwide specialist.

The published terms show what a buyer can expect. A new 2025 or 2026 vehicle can be financed from $10,000 to $150,000 for up to 72 months, up to 90% of its value. A used vehicle up to five years old runs $10,000 to $100,000 over 60 to 72 months. A used 2017 to 2020 vehicle runs $10,000 to $75,000 for up to 48 months. A $349 processing fee is charged upfront and included in the APR.

The contract still shows an annual percentage rate. Instalment sales of vehicles fall under the federal Truth in Lending Act, so the contract shows an APR even when the provider calls the margin a profit rate. Comparing offers by APR keeps the comparison honest.

A late payment does not make the price grow. Contracts may include late charges, and under Sharia practice those charges are usually donated to charity. The contract and the Sharia board approval should explain this. UIF lists vehicle refinancing as coming soon, while purchase and lease buyout financing are offered now.

Insurance is the gap in the market. No dedicated takaful operator sells US auto policies, so most Muslim drivers use conventional or mutual insurers while the car is financed. The instalment payments are generally reported to credit bureaus, so on-time payments can help build a credit history for a future home finance application, such as the home purchase plan the Nov 21 edition explains.

A dealer's zero-percent offer needs a careful read. A genuine zero-percent instalment price with no added interest may be acceptable to many scholars, but the contract usually contains late-payment interest clauses. Reading the retail instalment contract and asking about penalties comes before signing.
