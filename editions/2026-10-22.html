Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Thursday, October 22, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

🇲🇾 1. Malaysia's central bank launches a digital-ledger platform for tokenized bonds, including sukuk

Bank Negara Malaysia launched SSDS+, a platform built on distributed-ledger technology for issuing and holding tokenized bonds, including sukuk. The platform keeps the official record of who owns each security and how ownership changes hands, and it connects to the RENTAS+ payment system so bond payments settle in central bank money around the clock. The central bank built it for wholesale issuance first, but says it is also ready for tokenized government bonds and retail corporate bonds. No securities have been issued through it yet, and no volumes were disclosed.

What to learn: A depository is the official record of who owns a security. Today, that record sits with the central bank's securities depository, and settlement follows banking hours and multi-day cycles. SSDS+ moves that record onto a shared digital ledger run by the central bank itself, which is what makes round-the-clock settlement possible. When a bond changes hands, the token moves on the ledger and the cash leg settles at the same time in central bank money through RENTAS+ — so buyer and seller do not wait on each other's systems.

Tokenization changes the record-keeping. The contract behind the bond stays the same. A tokenized sukuk still needs the same asset-backed structure and Shariah approvals as a paper one — the Oct 7 edition explains how sukuk work. The technology matters most to the dealers and fund managers who trade Malaysian bonds every day, and later to ordinary investors when retail bonds go on the platform.

The ringgit is Malaysia's currency. A ringgit stablecoin is a digital token built to hold the same value as one ringgit. Malaysia's move follows a three-year tokenization roadmap the central bank published in November 2025 and ringgit-stablecoin trials it ran with Standard Chartered, CIMB, Maybank, and Capital A in February.

The USA could adopt the same approach through its own market systems. The Federal Reserve and the Treasury keep the official records for US government securities. A shared-ledger version of those records would let a sale and its payment settle at the same moment, at any hour. US Islamic platforms could then offer tokenized sukuk in the apps ordinary investors already use, with the same Shariah approvals a paper sukuk needs.

🇳🇬 2. Nigeria's alt bank moves all its services onto a core banking system built for Islamic finance

Alt bank, Nigeria's standalone non-interest bank, has finished moving all of its banking services onto MIZAN, a core banking platform developed by the Nigerian software firm Peerless specifically for Islamic and non-interest financial institutions. Managing Director Hassan Yusuf said the industry can no longer keep running on "workarounds" inside systems built for conventional banking. Chief Operating Officer Fatai Tella confirmed services are now live on the new platform. The migration follows the bank's decision earlier this year to join the International Islamic Financial Market, the body that writes standards for Islamic finance market practice.

What to learn: A core banking system is the software that records every account, payment, and financing deal a bank handles. Conventional cores assume a loan: principal goes out, interest accrues on a schedule. Islamic contracts need different records. A murabaha needs the bank to record owning an asset before selling it on. An ijara needs a rental schedule against an owned asset. A mudaraba needs a profit-sharing ratio and a pool of investor money whose returns are calculated and split. MIZAN was built with these contract shapes from the start, so products like Murabaha, Ijara, Mudaraba, Istisna, and Qard Hassan are configured from templates instead of being bent to fit loan screens. Transactions also pass a Shariah validation check before they hit the ledger, and the bank's Shariah reviewers can follow the trail through audit logs. For the bank's customers, the change means new products reach the market faster and existing ones run with fewer manual fixes behind the scenes. This is the same software mismatch that Islamic windows inside conventional banks live with every day — the Oct 21 edition covered how windows keep their books apart.

🔗 Source: The Sun Nigeria

🇲🇾 3. Bank Muamalat launches a credit card that never charges profit on profit

Bank Muamalat Malaysia launched the Awwal Credit Card-i on October 20, a Shariah-compliant credit card with a 14% annual profit rate, no annual fee, and a non-compounding profit concept. The card was officially launched by Deputy Prime Minister Fadillah Yusof at the 21st Kuala Lumpur Islamic Finance Forum. President and CEO Khairul Kamarudin said the card was built around responsible financing, and a launch promotion adds complimentary takaful coverage on outstanding balances. Applications opened the same day on the bank's website and at its branches.

What to learn: A credit card-i is a Shariah-compliant credit card. The difference from a conventional card comes down to how the charge works. Interest is a charge on money you borrowed. On a conventional card, each month's interest is added to what you owe. The next month, you are charged interest on that bigger amount. You end up paying interest on interest, and the debt grows on its own. This card charges a profit rate instead, and the profit is worked out only on the amount you actually spent and have not paid back yet. Profit from earlier months is never added to your balance, so the charge cannot grow by itself. Pay the statement in full and no profit is charged at all. Carry a balance and the 14% annual rate applies to that amount only. The takaful promotion covers the outstanding balance — takaful is the mutual-support principle the Oct 10 edition covered — so the debt is settled if something happens to the cardholder. The people this reaches are ordinary Malaysian cardholders managing monthly spending: a young professional who carries a balance for a few months pays less than she would on a card where charges compound.

Learn

Why banks cannot just bolt Islamic products onto old banking software

A conventional core banking system knows one shape of deal: money goes out as a loan, interest accrues on a schedule, the borrower repays. Islamic contracts do not fit that shape. In a murabaha, the bank must first record owning the asset, then record selling it at a disclosed markup. In an ijara, the bank owns the asset and the customer pays rent for its use. In a mudaraba there is no borrower. There is a pool of investor money, a profit-sharing ratio, and actual profits to calculate and split. Forcing these contracts through loan screens means staff keep parallel spreadsheets and reconcile by hand. That is slow, and every manual step is a place where a Shariah rule can be broken by accident. Purpose-built software like the MIZAN platform in today's second story removes the workarounds by recording the real contract from the start.
