Today's stories

🌍 1. A Bahrain startup launches live Shariah compliance checks for Islamic banks

SITR Compliance Technology, a startup based in Manama, Bahrain, has launched a platform that checks an Islamic bank's transactions against its Shariah board's rulings as they happen. The launch was announced during Gateway Gulf 2026, an investment forum hosted by the Bahrain Economic Development Board. The platform connects to a bank's core system through read-only links and can read contracts and scanned documents, so it can flag a wrong execution order, missing paperwork, or a change to core banking settings before the problem turns into income the bank cannot keep. Founder and chief executive Yousuf Tyabji said Shariah compliance has stayed manual while other industries moved to automation and machine learning. The company is still completing the final stage of its commercial licence in Bahrain and plans to serve banks across the Gulf.

What to learn: The change SITR brings is timing. Most banks today check a sample of transactions weeks or months after they happen. SITR checks every transaction as it happens, so a mistake is caught while it can still be fixed. The platform works without touching the bank's live systems. Its read-only connection watches the transactions and reads the documents, including scanned PDFs, without changing anything in the bank's core system. That matters because a bank cannot pause its daily operations to install new software. The platform covers the full product range, from Murabaha and Ijarah to Musharaka, Mudaraba, Wakala, and Sukuk. The change for an ordinary customer is indirect. The financing a customer uses is checked properly, and the bank keeps the trust of its Shariah board. In the USA, a bank in the same position uses conventional compliance software, and no Shariah compliance monitoring product of this kind is a standard product at US banks.

🌍 2. Pakistan's first digital Islamic bank adds Shariah-compliant phone financing

aik, Pakistan's first fully digital Islamic banking platform from BankIslami, has partnered with PalmPay Pakistan to offer Shariah-compliant smartphone financing through the aik app. Customers can browse financing options, submit documents, and receive a decision on their phone, then repay the cost of the phone in pre-agreed instalments. PalmPay Pakistan holds a buy-now-pay-later licence from Pakistan's Securities and Exchange Commission and says it uses the Shariah-compliant Musawamah model, with an independent Shariah Advisory Board overseeing the product. aik chief officer Ashfaque Ahmad said customers want financing that aligns with their faith, and PalmPay Pakistan chief executive Jun Zeng said owning a smartphone is the first step into the digital economy. No financing amounts or rates were disclosed.

What to learn: The financing rests on a sale. The customer buys the phone at an agreed price and pays it off in instalments, and the price is fixed in advance, so there is no interest charge and no surprise in the monthly amount. The closest related idea is Murabaha, where a bank buys an item and sells it to the customer at a disclosed markup, which was explained in the Oct 8 edition. PalmPay describes its own model as Musawamah, a sale at a price both sides agree without disclosing the seller's cost. The new part is the delivery. A fully digital Islamic bank and a device-financing specialist run the whole process inside an app, instead of through a branch visit. The human angle is the phone itself. For a worker in Pakistan, a phone is the tool for work, payments, and business, and the upfront cost is the barrier. The instalments spread that cost over time. In the USA, a buyer in the same position uses a carrier instalment plan or a credit card, and both charge interest.

🌍 3. A Maldives healthcare group raises MVR 80 million through a Parallel Istisna sukuk

Medtech Maldives plans to raise MVR 80 million (about $5.2 million) through a Shariah-compliant sukuk to build an oceanfront healthcare and commercial complex in Malé. The sukuk uses a Parallel Istisna structure and comes in two series: MVR 32 million (about $2.1 million) over seven years at an estimated 9.00% a year, and MVR 48 million (about $3.1 million) over nine years at 9.50%. Each certificate costs MVR 1,000 (about $65), the minimum subscription is MVR 10,000 (about $650) per series, and subscriptions run until November 30. The building will hold clinics, specialist medical facilities, and offices. Managing Director Mubah Rafeeu said the money will fund the facility and support the group's growth. First National Finance Corporation arranged the issue.

What to learn: Istisna is a contract to build something to order, and it was explained in the Oct 17 edition. Parallel Istisna is the new part, and it uses two Istisna contracts back to back. In the first contract, the financier agrees to deliver a finished building to the investors. In the second contract, the financier hires a builder to construct that building. Money from the sukuk pays the builder in stages as construction progresses, and the finished building satisfies the first contract. The return comes from the construction and delivery of a real building, and the profit rate is agreed in advance. The two series give investors a choice between a shorter term at 9.00% and a longer term at 9.50%. The people in the story are the patients and clinic staff who will use the building, and the investors whose money funds it. In the USA, a developer in the same position borrows from a bank at interest to build a clinic, and the sukuk route lets Maldivian investors fund a hospital without interest.

Shariah audit — how a bank checks its own compliance

A Shariah audit checks whether a bank's transactions followed the rulings of its Shariah board. The board is a group of scholars that sets the rules for what the bank may do. The audit checks the practice against those rules.

The audit exists because a product can be approved on paper and still go wrong in practice. A document can be signed in the wrong order, or a fee can be charged that the ruling did not allow. The audit catches the gap between the ruling and the transaction.

The check is usually done after the event. A bank's internal Shariah audit team reviews a sample of transactions weeks or months later and reports any that broke the rules. An external Shariah audit then reviews that work independently. SITR, covered in story 1 above, checks every transaction as it happens instead of sampling afterwards.

A simple example shows how a check works. In a Murabaha car purchase, the bank must buy the car first and then sell it to the customer. If the paperwork shows the customer bought the car before the bank did, the sequence is wrong and the transaction fails the check.

When a transaction fails, the income from it cannot be kept as profit. It is set aside for charity. That rule is what makes the audit matter. Without it, a bank could keep money earned from a transaction that broke its own Shariah rules.

The people affected are the customers and the bank. The customer gets financing that stays genuinely compliant, and the bank keeps the trust of its Shariah board and its customers.
