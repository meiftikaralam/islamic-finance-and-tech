What a sukuk order book tells you

A headline says a bank sold a $500 million sukuk and the order book was 2.4x covered. For a saver or learner reading that, here is what happened behind the headline.

When a bank sells a sukuk, it first publishes price guidance: the return it plans to pay, quoted as a spread over US Treasury bonds. A typical line reads "115 basis points over US Treasuries." A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 115 bps means 1.15 percentage points above the Treasury rate. Treasuries are used as the benchmark because they are the safest dollar asset — everything else is priced as a premium for taking more risk.

Investors then place their orders into the order book. If $500 million is on offer and $1.2 billion of orders arrive, the book is 2.4x covered. That is oversubscription: demand is more than double the supply. Strong demand lets the bank tighten the price, paying investors less than it first guided. The order book is a live poll of institutional confidence. A book that fills in hours signals deep demand for Shariah-compliant paper from pension funds, insurers, and banks.

The process for a bond sale and a sukuk sale are similar. Bookrunners collect orders, allocate the certificates, and settle through clearing systems. However, a sukuk is asset-backed. Investors hold claims on real assets, and their returns come from those assets.

Three numbers worth checking before you join a halal fund

A woman comparing two halal ETFs on her phone has dozens of tabs open and no idea which numbers matter. Three numbers carry most of the weight.

The first is the expense ratio: the annual fee, as a percentage of your assets. SPUS charges 0.45%, while a newer competitor charges 0.40%. That half of a tenth of a percent compounds every year for decades — fees are the one thing an investor controls.

The second is fund size. SPUS now holds about $3.36 billion. Size matters because larger funds are cheaper and easier to trade: tighter spreads between buy and sell prices, and big orders absorbed without moving the price. A tiny fund can be wide, thin, and costly to trade even with a low fee.

The third is the Shariah setup: who certifies the fund, and whether it publishes a yearly purification amount per share. Screening keeps most of a portfolio halal; purification handles the small remainder — the interest earned on cash balances between trades — by telling you exactly how much to donate. A fund that reports this number each year is doing the cleaning work for you.

These three factors play an important role in finding whether a fund can be trusted. They tell you what it costs, how tradable it is, and how its Shariah compliance is handled.
