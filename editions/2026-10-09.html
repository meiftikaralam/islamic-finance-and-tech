🇺🇸 1. Defense VC fund Sentinel Shield I appoints Al Marji Advisory for Sharia governance

MASNA Ventures' Sentinel Shield Fund I, a Saudi-regulated defense venture capital fund, has appointed Al Marji Advisory Company to provide independent Sharia governance, reports mid-east.info. The Riyadh-based fund manager j. awan capital runs the fund, which backs the local manufacture of proven US and Allied advanced technologies — from unmanned systems to AI-enabled defense — in Saudi Arabia. Al Marji Advisory will set up and supervise an independent Sharia board, review the fund's structure and investments, oversee certification, and run periodic Sharia audits.

What to learn: A Sharia board is a panel of Islamic scholars that checks whether a fund's deals follow Islamic rules. Here the fund hired an outside advisory firm to do the job instead of certifying itself, because an outside check carries more weight with investors. The mandate goes beyond a one-time stamp: the advisers review the fund's structure, each investment, and the special-purpose vehicles used to hold them, then run audits on a schedule for the life of the fund. This sits alongside the Saudi Capital Market Authority's regulation of the fund — religious oversight and financial regulation working side by side. The impact: even a defense-tech venture fund can now court Sharia-conscious investors, and it shows independent Sharia oversight spreading from banks into venture capital.

💡 2. GUIDANCE: Where US Muslims can park emergency cash without earning interest

This is an evergreen explainer, not news. A savings account pays you interest — which is riba, even when it is your own money growing. So the standard advice to keep three to six months of expenses in a high-yield savings account does not work as-is for Muslims. Workable options in the US: a non-interest-bearing checking account for instant access; profit-sharing savings accounts at Islamic banks such as Devon Bank in Chicago or University Islamic Financial in Michigan, which pay a share of profits instead of interest; or a short-term sukuk fund for cash you will not need immediately. Keep the true emergency portion liquid — money you might need next week should not sit in anything whose value can drop.

Why it matters for learners: Riba rules apply to money you receive, not just money you borrow. Checking where your savings returns come from is part of staying halal.

💡 3. GUIDANCE: What "accredited investor" means — and why it gates some funds

This is an evergreen explainer, not news. In the US, the SEC lets companies sell certain private investments only to "accredited investors": individuals earning $200,000 or more a year ($300,000 with a spouse) or holding over $1 million in assets, not counting their home. The idea is that wealthier investors can absorb losses and do their own homework. Everyone else is limited to public investments like stocks and ETFs, or funds specifically opened to non-accredited investors. That is why a halal fund opening to everyday investors at a $100 minimum was newsworthy — it removed a gate most Americans face.

Why it matters for learners: When you see a private fund you cannot join, this SEC rule is usually the reason. Knowing the rule helps you read fund announcements correctly.

💡 4. GUIDANCE: Zakat on your stocks and ETFs — the 2.5% rule

This is an evergreen explainer, not news. If you hold stocks or ETFs for a full lunar year and their value sits above the nisab — the minimum threshold, roughly the value of 85 grams of gold — you owe zakat: 2.5% of the market value of the shares. Cash dividends sitting in your account count too, so add them to the total before calculating. Note that purification (donating the small non-compliant slice of a fund's income to charity) is separate from zakat; a careful investor does both. Many US investors pick one date a year — often in Ramadan — and pay through a charity.

Why it matters for learners: Investing creates a zakat duty that salary earners never face. Setting a fixed yearly date keeps it from slipping.

💡 5. GUIDANCE: Ijara — the lease-to-own structure behind US halal home financing

This is an evergreen explainer, not news. Ijara means leasing. In a halal home purchase, the financier buys the house and leases it to you; each monthly payment has two parts — rent for living in the house, and a buyout payment that gradually transfers ownership to you. When the final payment clears, the house is fully yours. This differs from murabaha, covered in the Oct 8 edition: there the bank sells you the house at a marked-up price and you repay that fixed price. Both structures avoid interest; the difference is whether you are buying outright (murabaha) or renting toward ownership (ijara).

Why it matters for learners: US halal home financiers use both structures. Knowing which one your contract uses tells you how each payment is split and what happens if you sell early.

Learn Islamic Finance: Takaful

What it is: Takaful is Islamic insurance. Members contribute money to a shared pool, and when a member suffers a covered loss — a car accident, an illness, a death in the family — the pool pays out.

How it works: The pool runs on mutual help. Everyone pays a fixed contribution, and any money left over at the end of the year stays with the members. The company managing the pool (the takaful operator) earns a management fee or a share of the profits from investing the contributions — and those investments must be halal. Conventional insurance works differently: you buy a promise from a company, and the company keeps the profit when claims are low.

A real-world example: Family takaful works like life insurance. A parent contributes a fixed amount each month; if they pass away, the pool pays the family an agreed sum. In Malaysia and the Gulf, takaful companies sell these plans the way conventional insurers sell life policies — same shelf, different engine.
