🇬🇧 1. AlRayan Bank rebuilds its core banking on the cloud

AlRayan Bank has signed an agreement with Monument Technology to replace its core banking systems with a cloud-based platform, in what the bank calls its largest technology investment ever. The bank will run its savings, current accounts, property finance, payments and customer servicing on Monument's Banking Platform as a Service, known as Bank in a Box. The deal is Monument Technology's first move into Islamic banking and its first time covering current accounts. The new platform will be introduced in phases, and no investment figure was disclosed.

What to learn: A core banking system is the software that keeps a bank's central records. Every account, balance, payment and loan lives in it, and the app and the staff both read from the same records. A bank's products sit on top of the core system. If the core system cannot handle a product, the bank cannot offer it, or staff have to work around it by hand. Take a customer who opens a savings account at AlRayan. The core system records the deposit, tracks the profit share and shows the balance in the app. AlRayan's current systems are older, and replacing them is a large job because every account and every past transaction has to move across without errors. Banking Platform as a Service means the bank rents the platform instead of building and running its own. Monument hosts the software in the cloud and keeps it updated, and AlRayan uses it to run its products. The platform was first built for Monument Bank, a UK challenger bank, and Monument Technology became a separate venture in 2023 to sell it to other banks. Ecology Building Society and Castle Trust Bank have already chosen it, and Monument Technology closed a seed funding round of more than £18 million, about $23.7 million, in June 2026. The platform is modular, so the bank can upgrade one part, such as payments, without replacing the whole system. For AlRayan, the platform has to be adapted for Shariah-compliant products, including its property finance. A conventional core system is built around interest-bearing loans, so an Islamic bank needs the system to record who owns an asset at each step, rental payments and profit shares instead. The Oct 24 edition compared specific Shariah banking software products. AlRayan's chief executive, Giles Cunningham, said the bank has customer relationships built over more than twenty years and the new technology will match the digital banking customers expect. Monument Technology's chief executive, Steve Britain, said the platform lets banks modernise without building and maintaining everything themselves. For customers, the bank says it will be able to launch new products faster and improve its digital banking. A customer applying for home purchase finance should see a smoother online process, and staff should spend less time working around old systems.

🌍 2. Emirates funds two A350s with a Shariah-compliant French lease

Emirates, Dubai Islamic Bank and Natixis Corporate and Investment Banking have completed a 12-year Shariah-compliant French lease to fund Emirates' purchase of two new Airbus A350-900 aircraft in 2026. The deal brings together two financing frameworks that are usually arranged separately, fitting the features of a French lease to Shariah principles in a single structure. Dubai Islamic Bank and Natixis acted as Islamic financiers and arrangers, and Dubai Islamic Bank also structured the Shariah side of the deal. Emirates' acting chief financial officer, Derek Blaney, said the deal sets a precedent for future aircraft financing. No financing amount was disclosed.

What to learn: A French lease is a tax-driven way to pay for an aircraft. Tax-driven means the main reason for the structure is to save tax, not just to buy the plane. A leasing company owned by French companies that pay French corporate tax buys the plane. These owners are banks and large companies, not the public and not the government. They put money into the leasing company as tax investors because the structure cuts their tax bills. The saving comes from French tax rules on depreciation. Depreciation is the yearly write-down of the plane's value for tax purposes. The normal rule spreads the cost evenly over the plane's life. The French rules let the leasing company write the value down much faster in the early years. The leasing company passes its yearly result straight to its owners, so the paper loss lands on the owners' own tax bills. The fast write-down creates a large loss on paper, and that loss cuts the tax the owners pay on their other profits. The saving is the tax on that extra write-down, nothing else. The total write-down over the plane's life is the same either way. The benefit is timing: the tax saving arrives early, when the write-downs are biggest. Take a plane that costs $100 million. Spreading the cost evenly over 20 years gives a write-down of $5 million a year. That is $100 million divided by 20. The fast French write-down for a 20-year plane is 12.5% of the remaining value each year. In year one that is $12.5 million. The extra write-down in year one is $12.5 million minus $5 million, which is $7.5 million. French corporate tax is 25%, so the extra write-down cuts the owners' tax bill by $7.5 million times 25%, which is about $1.9 million in that one year. Part of that saving reaches the airline as lower rent. The planes in this deal are new, and the French rules only allow the fast write-down for a new aircraft. In this deal, the leasing company owns the two planes during the lease, and Emirates pays rent to use them. The announcement does not name the leasing company or the tax investors behind it. What the announcement does name is Dubai Islamic Bank and Natixis as the Islamic financiers and arrangers, with Dubai Islamic Bank also structuring the Shariah side. At around year five or six, the airline can buy the shares of the leasing company for a nominal price and take over the planes. The closest Shariah structure is ijara, a lease where the owner rents out a real asset and the rent pays for its use. The French lease and ijara rest on the same ground: the lessor must own the asset and carry the risks of ownership, and the rent pays for the use of the plane. (Whether the Shariah side of this deal used ijara is not confirmed in the announcement.) Interest-bearing loans cannot sit inside the structure because interest is riba. A Shariah-compliant deal cannot include a loan that charges interest. The money has to flow through ownership and rent on a real asset instead. In a conventional French lease, the senior loan charges interest, so that part cannot stay as it is in a Shariah version. Dubai Islamic Bank's Shariah team checked each part of the French lease against Shariah standards and adjusted the structure where the two sets of rules did not match. The announcement does not say which parts had to change. The USA can do similar things in two ways. A US airline can use a French tax lease, because the USA has a tax treaty with France that waives the withholding tax on the payments. The USA also has its own version, the leveraged lease. A US leasing company owns the plane and writes it down fast under US tax rules, which allow 20% of the cost in the first year and 32% in the second. The early write-downs are bigger than the rent the company receives, so it shows a loss on paper and pays less tax on its other profits. Part of the saving reaches the airline as lower rent. Take the same $100 million plane. In year one the US write-down is $20 million. At the 21% US corporate tax rate, that write-down cuts the owner's tax bill by $4.2 million in that year. The deal runs for 12 years and covers two A350-900 aircraft, the long-range jets Emirates is adding to its fleet. For an ordinary investor, the lesson is that Islamic finance now reaches into large cross-border deals that used to run on conventional structures only. A structure built for French tax rules can be reshaped to meet Shariah rules, and the result can be used again for other airlines and other assets. The passengers boarding those two jets will fly on planes financed without interest, and the rent Emirates pays flows to investors through a lease on a real aircraft.
