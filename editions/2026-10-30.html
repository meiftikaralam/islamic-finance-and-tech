Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Friday, October 30, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

Today's stories

🌍 1. Pakistan opens a stock exchange counter that trades only Shariah-compliant securities

The Pakistan Stock Exchange has launched a Shariah-compliant trading counter, which went live on October 29. Trading on the counter is limited to Shariah-compliant equities, Government Ijarah Sukuk, and corporate sukuk. Thirty-nine brokers have joined to offer Shariah-compliant trading through Islamic windows, and two more brokerage houses hold licences as full Shariah-compliant brokers. The exchange also opened a Shariah Portal listing every Shariah-eligible stock and sukuk. At the launch, the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan said 309 of the exchange's 537 listed companies are Shariah-compliant, covering 65% of the market's value, and that Islamic mutual funds have grown to Rs22 trillion (roughly $79 billion) from Rs17 trillion (roughly $61 billion).

What to learn: A trading counter is a separate section inside an existing stock exchange, with its own rules for what can trade there. This counter exists because a Pakistani investor who wants a fully halal portfolio faced a practical problem. The investor could pick Shariah-compliant stocks one by one, and still end up using conventional margin lending or holding a non-compliant stock by mistake. The counter removes that problem at the system level. The exchange's systems block non-compliant securities, short selling, and derivatives on the counter. Interest-based leverage is blocked too. The conventional Margin Trading System lets an investor borrow money from a broker to buy more shares, and the loan carries interest. That product is switched off on the counter. Murabaha Share Financing is offered in its place. Murabaha was covered as a concept in the Oct 8 edition. In this version, the financier buys shares and sells them to the investor at a disclosed markup, paid over time, so the investor gets financing for a share purchase without an interest-bearing loan. The people who run the counter are the exchange, the regulator, and the brokers together. The exchange sets the procedures, the regulator approved the rules, and brokers offer the service through Islamic windows or as full Shariah brokers. The change reaches an ordinary investor directly. A small investor in Karachi can open an account, including a Sahulat Account with simpler account-opening forms, and trade from a list the exchange itself publishes, instead of screening every stock alone. In the USA, an investor in the same position uses a screening app such as Zoya and a conventional brokerage account, because no US exchange runs a separate Shariah-only trading counter.

🌍 2. Zetrix confirms a dissolution event on its sukuk after the shareholder covenant breach

Zetrix AI Berhad has confirmed that a dissolution event has been triggered under its Sukuk Wakalah programme of up to RM2.0 billion, after the covenant requiring its founder, Wong Thean Soon, to remain the single largest shareholder was breached. The confirmation was filed on October 29 in reply to a Bursa Malaysia query. The company stressed that it is not currently required to immediately repay the full outstanding amount, and that it has met all scheduled payments so far. The company did not disclose the amount actually outstanding under the programme, or the exact shareholding percentages. No resolution was passed at the October 23 sukukholders' meeting, and the date of the next meeting has not been fixed. The original covenant breach was covered in the Oct 18 edition.

What to learn: A dissolution event is a trigger written into a sukuk's terms. It fires when a defined adverse event happens, and it gives the trustee and the sukukholders contractual rights that did not apply before. The trigger here is a control-stability covenant. The covenant required one named person to remain the single largest shareholder, so that control of the company stays stable while the sukuk is outstanding. When that condition broke, the dissolution event fired. The next question is what changes for a holder. A dissolution event is not the same as a payment default. Zetrix says it has paid everything due on schedule, and a payment default would arise only if it fails to repay the full amount when that amount becomes due and payable. What the event does change is the holders' position. The trustee and holders now hold rights under the triggered terms, including the possibility of seeking accelerated repayment, and the company has not said whether holders or the trustee may declare the sukuk immediately due and payable. That question is still open, because no resolution was passed at the October 23 meeting and no next meeting date is fixed. For a sukukholder, the practical position is a waiting position. Scheduled payments continue, the existing terms stay in force, and the next development to watch is a waiver, a cure plan for the covenant breach, or a demand from the trustee or holders. In the USA, a bondholder in the same position watches the same kind of trigger, called an event of default clause in a bond indenture, and waits for the trustee to act.

🤖 AI watch

🌍 Mal, an AI-native Islamic platform, describes a mortgage decision in three seconds

Mal, a UAE-based Islamic financial platform built around artificial intelligence, set out its model in an interview published on October 28. Founder Abdallah Abu-Sheikh said most banks run digitisation programmes from the 2000s on fragmented systems, while Mal is built from scratch on clean, coherent data, with risk, compliance, legal, and back-office work running at machine speed. The claim at the centre of the interview is that a mortgage decision taking 30 days at a traditional bank could take three seconds at Mal. Mal opened a global waitlist in September 2026, three months after receiving in-principle approval from the Central Bank of the UAE, and it raised $230 million in seed funding led by BlueFive Capital. Mal is still pre-launch. It holds no banking or financial services licence yet, and it is not conducting regulated financial activities.

What to learn: AI-native means artificial intelligence is built into the core of the platform from the start, rather than added to an existing system later. The distinction matters because of how a traditional bank's systems grew. A traditional bank runs separate systems for accounts, loans, compliance checks, and payments, built at different times, and staff move information between them. That is one reason a mortgage decision takes weeks. Mal's claim is that a single data foundation removes those handoffs. Risk checks, compliance checks, and document checks run on the same data at the same time, so the decision comes back in seconds. The interview gives one guardrail for that speed. AI at Mal can never act without user verification, and it cannot bypass risk, compliance, or security parameters. That guardrail is the part a reader should weigh most carefully, because a three-second decision still has to satisfy the same affordability and identity checks as a thirty-day decision. The platform is aimed at underbanked communities, starting in the UAE and expanding across the Middle East and Asia. For a person waiting weeks for a financing decision, the change would be the wait itself. The status matters for the reader. Mal has in-principle approval and a waitlist, and its first products have not launched, so the three-second mortgage is a stated design goal, and the live product is still to come. In the USA, a borrower in the same position uses a conventional digital mortgage lender for speed, and a Shariah-compliant version of that instant decision is not a standard product at US banks.
