Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Saturday, November 7, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

Today's stories

🌍 1. Two Malaysian sukuk hit trouble in the same week

MARC Ratings downgraded two Malaysian sukuk programmes on November 6, and the two failures are different kinds. Zetrix AI's RM2 billion (about $490 million) sukuk programme was cut to a default rating of D-IS, after the company failed to settle a RM25 million (about $6.1 million) obligation that fell due that day. MARC had already cut the programme to C-IS on October 30, and it said it will no longer provide analytical coverage on Zetrix AI. YNH Property's RM700 million (about $172 million) Sukuk Wakalah programme was cut to B-IS from BB-IS, after a shortfall in the company's sukuk reserve account triggered a technical default. Zetrix's slide was covered in the Oct 30 edition and the Nov 2 edition.

What to learn: Zetrix missed a payment. Money that was due on a set date did not arrive, so the rating agency now treats the sukuk as in default. The next scheduled payment of RM214 million (about $52.5 million) is not due until March 2027, so the RM25 million that was missed is a separate obligation that came due first. YNH has not missed a payment. It fell short on the amount it was required to hold in a reserve account by a set date. As of October 27 the account held RM54.4 million (about $13.3 million), which was RM6.8 million (about $1.7 million) short of the RM61.2 million (about $15 million) required, and the shortfall must be fixed within a 30-day remedy period. A reserve account is a pot of money the issuer must build up before a maturity date, so the cash is already there when the repayment falls due. The same idea exists in the USA, where corporate bonds often carry a sinking fund rule that works the same way. The people holding Zetrix's sukuk are mostly fund managers and banks, but funds run by AmanahRaya Investment Management hold more than RM500 million (about $123 million) and less than RM600 million (about $147 million) of it, which is money belonging to ordinary Malaysian investors who invested through those funds. Zetrix is also under pressure from other directions. Its subsidiary MyEG was suspended as a collection agent for the Road Transport Department after failing to pass on RM314 million (about $77 million) collected for the government, and MyEG faces a RM353.13 million (about $86.5 million) tax bill. The founder, TS Wong, has seen his stake fall from nearly 30% to 1.84% after forced share sales, and the share price has fallen more than 90% since late August. YNH's repayment depends on two land sales completing on time. The first is a RM170 million sale of a 5.1-acre plot in Desa Sri Hartamas to a Sunway unit, which has been delayed since May 2023 and must meet its remaining conditions by December 31. After repaying about RM65 million (about $15.9 million) of bank loans on the land, about RM105 million (about $25.7 million) would go toward the sukuk. The second is a RM455 million (about $111.5 million) sale of land on Jalan Sultan Ismail to a Chin Hin Group unit, expected to complete by January 2027, with the proceeds set aside to redeem a RM350 million (about $85.8 million) perpetual bond. In the USA, a company that misses a bond payment is also treated as in default, and the bondholders can act through a trustee to claim what they are owed.

What happens when a sukuk fails

A sukuk fails when the issuer cannot keep the promises in the sukuk agreement. The failure can take two forms, and today's story shows one of each.

The first form is a missed payment. The issuer does not pay an amount that was due on a set date. Zetrix AI, covered in story 1 above, missed a RM25 million obligation due on November 6, and MARC Ratings moved its sukuk to a default rating. A missed payment is the clearest kind of failure, because the money was due and it did not arrive.

The second form is a technical default. The issuer breaks a rule in the agreement while still paying on time. YNH Property, covered in story 1 above, fell short on the amount it had to hold in a reserve account by a set date. The payments were still being made. The breach was about the safety buffer. A technical default usually comes with a remedy period, and YNH has 30 days to top up the account.

A dissolution event sits between these two. It is a declaration that the agreement has been broken in a serious way, and it gives holders the right to demand their money back. Zetrix's dissolution event was covered in the Oct 30 edition. A dissolution event on its own does not force immediate repayment, and the next scheduled payment can still be months away. The missed payment is what turned Zetrix's situation into a default rating.

The holders decide what happens next through a trustee. The trustee calls the holders together, and they can agree to give the issuer more time, accept new terms, or demand repayment. For Zetrix, a meeting on October 23 ended without an agreement, and no new meeting date has been announced. For YNH, the holders are waiting to see whether the reserve shortfall is fixed inside the remedy period and whether the two land sales complete in time.

The money usually comes from selling assets. An issuer that cannot pay from its own income sells land, buildings, or businesses and uses the proceeds to repay holders. YNH's repayment depends on two land sales worth RM170 million and RM455 million. When the sales are delayed, the repayment is delayed with them, and the rating agency watches the timetable closely.

The people affected are the holders, and many of them are ordinary investors. Funds run by AmanahRaya Investment Management hold more than RM500 million of Zetrix's sukuk, and that money belongs to Malaysians who invested through those funds. A sukuk failure does not wipe out the underlying assets, and the holders have a claim on them, but getting the money back takes time, and the amount recovered depends on what the assets sell for.

For a beginner, the point to remember is simple. A sukuk is a claim on real assets and real income, and the agreement sets out what must happen and by when. When the issuer breaks those terms, the holders have rights, a trustee speaks for them, and the recovery comes from the assets behind the sukuk.
