Today's stories

🌍 1. CIMB Islamic puts investing, zakat and inheritance planning in one app

CIMB Islamic launched a new Islamic wealth management service on November 17. The service sits inside the bank's CIMB OCTO app and covers four areas the bank calls Grow, Protect, Purify and Distribute. It brings Shariah-compliant investing, takaful protection, zakat calculation and estate planning together in one place.

What to learn: The service is a single place for four jobs that Malaysian Muslim customers used to handle separately. Grow covers investing and saving. Protect covers takaful, which is insurance run on Islamic rules. Purify covers zakat, the yearly giving on wealth. Distribute covers passing wealth to heirs.

Three digital tools do the work. MyWealth has a Shariah selector that reads a customer's risk profile and suggests an asset mix from 57 Shariah-compliant funds. Faraid360 simulates how an estate would be divided under Islamic inheritance rules, and shows how a will, a lifetime gift, or a takaful payout would change the result. Zakat365 Lite estimates the zakat owed from the balances a customer holds with CIMB, and the bank plans to extend it to other investments later.

The bank's chief executive, Ahmad Shahriman, said customers often knew planning mattered and still put it off because the pieces were hard to follow. The numbers show the delay. About RM90 billion, around $21.9 billion, in Muslim estates in Malaysia sits unclaimed, up 135% since 2009, with 46,000 cases still waiting in the small-estate process. A family that never writes a will leaves its heirs waiting years for money that is already theirs.

CIMB Islamic runs the service, and the bank plans to grow its team of certified Islamic financial planners to 100 by the first quarter of 2027. It also aims to more than double its Islamic wealth assets under management by 2030. Sun Life Malaysia Takaful provides the protection cover, including a plan that pays cash to a family within two weeks of a death.

For a reader in the USA, the comparison is direct. An American Muslim buys these pieces separately: a screening app such as Zoya, a managed portfolio from Wahed, and estate planning tools from a firm such as ShariaWiz, as covered in the Nov 16 edition. CIMB puts all four in one bank app. In the USA, the pieces stay separate, and no bank there offers all four in one app.

How Islamic inheritance divides an estate

When a Muslim dies, Islamic law divides the estate by fixed shares. The shares are set in the Quran, and the family does not choose them. The shares depend on who survives: a wife, children, parents, and other relatives each have a set fraction.

Take a simple case. A man in Kuala Lumpur dies leaving RM240,000, a wife, a son and a daughter. His wife receives one-eighth, RM30,000, because children survive him. The remaining RM210,000 goes to the children, and a son receives twice a daughter's share. The son receives RM140,000 and the daughter receives RM70,000. The three shares add to RM240,000.

Two tools let a person shape the result while alive. A wasiat, a will, can give up to one-third of the estate to people outside the fixed shares, such as a charity or a friend. The other two-thirds still follow the fixed shares. A hibah, a gift made during life, moves an asset out of the estate at once, so it is not divided at all.

The shares are fixed, and the paperwork still takes time. An estate needs an executor to settle debts, collect the assets and pay each heir. When no will names one, the family must ask a court to appoint an administrator, and the wait can run into years. In Malaysia, about RM90 billion in Muslim estates sits unclaimed, with 46,000 cases waiting in the small-estate process. Faraid360, the tool in today's first story, runs the shares for a person's own family before death, so the family can see the numbers and write the will.

In the USA, no law sets these shares. A Muslim family there writes a will and sets up trusts under state law, and the documents carry out the same shares. Firms such as ShariaWiz build those documents into their service, as covered in the Nov 16 edition.
