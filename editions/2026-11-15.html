Zetrix AI tells Bursa Malaysia it cannot repay its RM2 billion sukuk, and asks holders to rewrite the deal

By Iftikar · November 15, 2026 · 7 min read

IN THIS EDITION

Zetrix AI tells Bursa Malaysia it cannot repay its RM2 billion sukuk, and asks holders to rewrite the deal — plus what a sukuk trustee does for holders.

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

Today's stories

🌍 1. Zetrix AI tells Bursa Malaysia it cannot repay its RM2 billion sukuk

Zetrix AI has told Bursa Malaysia it cannot repay the full RM2 billion (about $490 million) outstanding under its Sukuk Wakalah programme. The disclosure, made on November 13 in response to a Bursa Malaysia directive, is the most detailed public account yet of the company's position. It also confirmed a further missed payment of RM25 million (about $6.1 million) due on November 6, and said it plans to repay that amount before calling another meeting of sukukholders. No date for that meeting has been fixed.

The company has put two changes to its financing terms to the holders. The first widens the rule that managing director Wong Thean Soon must remain the company's largest shareholder, so that Wong and parties acting together with him can hold that position as a group. His stake has fallen to 1.84% after forced share sales, down from 29.25% in April. The second change would let the company pledge up to 10% of its net assets as security for new financing, with the money raised held in a separate escrow account to meet sukuk repayments. Neither proposal has been accepted yet. The company also said it has withheld RM49.76 million (about $12 million) of its 2025 final dividend, and that losing its Road Transport Department collection business is expected to cut revenue by about 15%.

What to learn: This story is the next chapter of the Zetrix failure covered in the Nov 7 edition, which explained what happens when a sukuk fails. The new question is what happens after a borrower says it cannot pay.

A sukuk is a contract, and the contract has promises in it called covenants. One promise in Zetrix's deal was that Wong Thean Soon would stay the company's largest shareholder. When forced sales of his pledged shares cut his stake to 1.84%, that promise broke. A broken promise does not by itself force the company to repay everything at once. It gives the holders the right to demand changes, and the company must sit down with them and negotiate.

That negotiation is what the November 13 disclosure describes. Zetrix is asking the holders to rewrite the broken promise, so that Wong and people acting with him count together as the largest shareholder. It is also asking for permission to borrow against up to 10% of its net assets, with the money kept in an escrow account that can only be used to pay sukukholders. An escrow account is a separate account controlled by a third party, so the money cannot be spent on anything else. Both proposals are still under discussion, and the holders have accepted neither.

The people this touches are the sukukholders, and behind them the ordinary Malaysians whose savings sit in the funds that hold the paper. Funds managed by AmanahRaya Investment Management hold more than RM500 million of the programme. A Malaysian retiree whose pension fund bought Zetrix sukuk is waiting on the same negotiation as the big institutions. The total at stake is RM2 billion in principal plus RM164.5 million (about $40 million) in future payments, about $529 million in all.

AmanahRaya Investment Management, which holds more than RM500 million of the programme, has now valued its Zetrix sukuk at zero in four of its funds, effective November 10. That does not mean the money is gone for good. The funds keep their right to claim repayment, and any money recovered later goes back into the funds. Dealings in the four funds stay suspended until November 23.

The lesson for an ordinary investor is that a sukuk default unfolds in stages. The missed RM25 million payment in November, the RM214 million (about $52.5 million) payment due in March 2027, the RM314 million (about $77 million) owed to the Road Transport Department, and a RM353.13 million (about $86.5 million) tax bill the company says it will contest all sit in the same negotiation. The holders' trustee calls the meetings and puts the proposals to a vote. Until the holders agree to new terms, the old terms stay in force, and the company keeps operating while it tries to find the money.

Learn

The sukuk trustee: the person who speaks for the holders

A sukuk can have hundreds of holders, from pension funds to ordinary investors. When something goes wrong, those holders cannot each negotiate with the borrower on their own. The sukuk trustee does that job for them.

Take a Malaysian schoolteacher whose pension fund holds Zetrix sukuk. She will never meet the company's directors, and she cannot call a meeting of holders by herself. The trustee can. In the Zetrix case the trustee is Pacific Trustees Berhad, a Malaysian trust company registered with the Securities Commission Malaysia. It called the October 23 meeting where holders discussed the broken shareholder covenant, and it will call the next meeting once Zetrix repays the overdue RM25 million.

The trustee's job starts before any trouble. When a sukuk is issued, the trustee holds the assets behind it on behalf of all the holders, and it checks that the borrower keeps the promises in the contract. If a promise breaks, the trustee tells the holders and calls a meeting. At the meeting the holders vote on what to do: accept new terms, demand repayment, or give the borrower more time. The trustee carries out whatever the holders decide.

The trustee is independent of the borrower. The company pays its fee, as the contract requires, but its duty runs to the holders under the trust deed and the regulator's rules. That independence matters most when the borrower is in trouble, because the trustee is the one who can demand payment, declare a dissolution event, or take the borrower to court if the holders vote for it. In the Zetrix case the holders have not yet voted on the two new proposals, so the trustee's next step is to call the meeting once the overdue payment is made.

For an ordinary investor, the trustee is the name to look for in a sukuk's offer document. It is the party that stands between the investor and the borrower when the deal goes wrong. A sukuk with a named, regulated trustee gives holders a way to act together. Without one, each holder would have to fight alone.
