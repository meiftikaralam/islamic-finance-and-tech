Today's stories

🌍 1. Wafr launches a blockchain that puts the whole sukuk lifecycle on one record

Dubai-based Islamic fintech Wafr has launched WafrChain, a private permissioned blockchain that brings sukuk issuance, distribution, settlement and profit payments onto one shared platform, with sukuk tokenisation live from day one. The launch comes with a pre-seed funding round backed by Coen Jonker, co-founder of Tyme Group, and Neal Cross, former chief innovation officer of DBS Bank. Wafr is led by chief executive Mahmoud Bouchelif, a former Islamic banker, and chief product officer Anthony Murphy, who has launched digital banking products at AMP, Commonwealth Bank of Australia and Macquarie Bank. The size of the pre-seed round was not disclosed.

What to learn: A sukuk is one of the hardest instruments in Islamic finance to bring to market. A single issuance usually passes through eight separate parties: the issuer, the trustee, the arrangers, the Shariah board, the custodians, the paying agent, the distributors and the investors. Each party keeps its own record of the same instrument. Every transfer, profit payment and corporate action is typed in again and checked against the other records. That re-keying and reconciling adds cost, delay and the risk of error, and a single issuance can take many months, sometimes more than a year, to reach the market. WafrChain gives all eight parties one shared record instead. The sukuk's terms, including the unit price, the profit schedule and the maturity, sit on the record next to the approval of the issuer's own Shariah board, recorded before distribution. Wafr's own Shariah board works with each issuer to confirm that the structure is compliant. Tokenisation means the sukuk's ownership is recorded as digital units on that record. Smart contracts route the profit payments automatically, so the money moves when the schedule says it should, without a person re-keying each payment. Institutions settle in ordinary currency through local real-time settlement systems and SWIFT, or in stablecoins, and they connect to the platform through a single API. The blockchain is private and permissioned, so only approved institutions join, and each participant can see only its own activity, keeping positions private from one another. Transactions reach finality in 300 milliseconds, and the network is built to keep running with only a third of its nodes active. For the investor waiting on a profit payment, the payment should arrive faster and with fewer errors. For the issuer, the cost and the delay of bringing a sukuk to market should fall. The market Wafr is aiming at is large: sukuk outstanding stood at $1.14 trillion in 2025, with annual issuance of $291.4 billion, up 14.5% on the year before, and global Islamic finance assets reached $6.5 trillion in 2025, up 9%. Wafr's roadmap goes further than sukuk, with fund tokenisation, tokenised deposits, trade finance and business-to-business settlement planned for institutions, and global accounts, cross-border remittances and cards planned for individuals. For readers in the USA, the platform is built for institutions first, and Wafr has not announced a US launch or a product an ordinary investor can use yet.

Stablecoins: digital money that holds a steady value

A stablecoin is a digital token designed to hold a steady value, usually one token equal to one US dollar. The best-known ones, such as Tether's USDT and Circle's USDC, are backed by reserves. For each token in circulation, the issuer holds a dollar or short-term US government debt in reserve. A person buys one stablecoin for one dollar, and redeeming the token returns the dollar. The token moves on a blockchain, so a payment can arrive in minutes at low cost, while a bank transfer across borders can take days. That speed is why Wafr plans to let institutions settle sukuk profit payments in stablecoins as well as in ordinary currency. The peg depends on the reserves actually being there. If an issuer's reserves fall short, the token can lose its value, and some stablecoins have already lost their peg. Regulators in the USA and elsewhere are writing rules for how issuers must hold and report their reserves. Scholars differ on whether a given stablecoin is Shariah-compliant, and the answer depends on what backs it and how it is used. Take an investor in Malaysia receiving a sukuk profit payment from a Gulf issuer. Paid in a stablecoin, the money can land in the investor's wallet in minutes. Paid through the usual bank channels, the same payment can take days to clear.
