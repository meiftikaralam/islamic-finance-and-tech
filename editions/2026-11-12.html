Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Thursday, November 12, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

Today's stories

🌍 1. A $100 million tokenized vault opens Saudi small-business loans to global investors

ZIG Finance launched the zEMS Vault on November 11, 2026, a fund that gives institutional investors exposure to a pool of loans made to small and medium-sized businesses in Saudi Arabia. The vault targets a $100 million raise, runs on the BNB Chain blockchain, and lets investors subscribe and redeem in stablecoins. The loans are originated by Beehive, a lending platform regulated by the Dubai Financial Services Authority. Zamanat structures the product and provides Shariah advisory, Laser Digital, the digital-assets arm of Japan's Nomura, advises on the risk framework and tokenization, and KAIO runs the tokenization and administration. ZIG Finance co-founder Abdul Rafay Gadit said the aim is to make the credit risk transparent and manageable. Laser Digital co-founder and chief executive Dr. Jez Mohideen called the launch an early test of whether private credit can be brought onchain in a form institutions can approve. The vault is open only to eligible institutional investors, and it excludes US persons and residents of Canada and Australia.

What to learn: A small shop in Riyadh needs money to buy stock. Its bank can take weeks to decide, and it often says no. This vault is a second way for money to reach that shop.

The money comes from big investors around the world. They put money into one shared pool. The pool holds loans to many small Saudi businesses at once, so one investor is not tied to one shop.

Beehive makes the loans. It checks each business, lends the money, and collects the repayments. It has done this since 2014, and it has lent more than $1 billion to over 2,100 small businesses in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Oman.

Each investor's share of the pool is recorded as a digital token. A token here is a record in a shared computer system that shows who owns which share. Investors pay in and cash out with digital dollars called stablecoins, which the Nov 10 edition covered. Money moves in minutes instead of days. ZIG Markets hands out the tokens. A platform called KAIO keeps the records and checks the identity of each investor when money goes in or out.

A firm called Zamanat checks the setup against Islamic rules. That check is what lets Muslim investors in the Gulf take part, since they cannot join a pool that earns interest in the usual way. Another firm, Khalij, is expected to join later to certify that the product follows Shariah rules.

The market behind this is large. Lending to Saudi small businesses reached about $125 billion at the end of 2025, up 33% in a year. Banks provide about 95% of it, and small businesses across the Gulf get less than 10% of bank lending. The gap between what these businesses need and what banks give them is more than $250 billion.

The risk is the same as in any lending. If a shop does not repay, the loss falls on the pool. There is no guarantee of returns, the money put in is not protected, and the pool is not a bank deposit. The loans take months or years to be repaid, so an investor who wants out quickly in a bad market may have to wait.

The pool is open only to big institutions, and it is closed to US persons, so an ordinary investor in the USA cannot buy in. The US link is on the company side. Laser Digital, the Nomura arm advising on this vault, received conditional approval in May 2026 to open a trust bank in the USA. The vault is also the first product from the partnership the two firms formed in August 2026. Laser Digital put in about $8 million at the start, and the two aim to build at least $100 million of these pools. ZIG Markets says it has originated more than $50 million in loans so far, with no defaults.

Private credit: lending that does not go through a bank

A fund collects money from investors, lends it to companies, and passes the repayments back to the investors. The companies that borrow are usually too small or too risky for a bank loan, and too small to issue bonds. A small business owner in Riyadh who needs money to buy stock or equipment is a typical borrower. The market has grown fast. After the 2008 financial crisis, banks pulled back from riskier lending and funds stepped in. Moody's puts global private credit at more than $2 trillion, and expects it to approach $4 trillion by 2030. Private credit also sits behind today's first story: the zEMS Vault is a tokenized private credit fund, and the Oct 7 edition covered a Shariah-compliant private credit fund from StepStone and ASB Capital.

A Shariah-compliant version cannot lend at interest. The financing has to be tied to a real asset or a real trade. One way is a cost-plus sale: the lender buys the equipment the business needs and sells it to the business at a markup, paid in instalments. Another way is a lease: the lender owns the asset and rents it to the business, as covered in the Oct 16 edition. A third way is profit-sharing, where the lender shares in the profit of the venture. The return comes from the asset or the trade. In a conventional loan, the return is interest charged on the money lent.

Tokenization changes how the fund is run. The fund's shares become tokens on a blockchain, and investors can subscribe and redeem in stablecoins. The money moves faster and the records are shared. The borrower still has to repay, and the lender still has to judge whether the borrower can. The risks stay with the lender. The borrower may not repay. The loans are hard to sell quickly, so an investor who wants out may have to wait. And the returns carry no guarantee. In the USA, most private credit funds lend at interest, so a Muslim investor looking for a Shariah-compliant version has few options. The funds that do exist are usually open only to institutions and wealthy individuals.
