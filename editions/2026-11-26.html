Dubai Islamic Bank agrees a $159bn all-share deal for Emirates Islamic

By Iftikar · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

IN THIS EDITION

Dubai Islamic Bank agrees a $159bn all-share deal for Emirates Islamic — plus what a bank takeover means, and how advisors build halal portfolios.

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

Today's stories

🌍 1. Dubai Islamic Bank agrees to buy Emirates Islamic in a $159bn all-share deal

Dubai Islamic Bank agreed on Wednesday, November 25, to buy Emirates Islamic, the Islamic banking arm of Emirates NBD, in an all-share deal. Dubai Islamic Bank will issue about 3.48 billion new shares to Emirates NBD, which will own 32.5% of the enlarged bank. The combined bank would hold about AED 585 billion (about $159 billion) in assets, making it the UAE's largest Islamic banking group and the world's second-largest listed Islamic banking group, behind Saudi Arabia's Al Rajhi Bank. The deal still needs shareholder approval and clearance from the Central Bank of the UAE and the Capital Markets Authority, and no completion date has been given.

What to learn: An all-share deal means the buyer pays with its own shares instead of cash. Dubai Islamic Bank hands Emirates NBD about 3.48 billion new shares, and in return it takes full ownership of Emirates Islamic. Emirates NBD keeps a 32.5% stake in the bigger bank.

Banks merge for scale and for deposits. A bank lends out the money its customers deposit, so a bank that wants to lend more needs more deposits. In the first nine months of 2026, Dubai Islamic Bank's financing grew nearly 10% while its deposits rose just 1%, and its loans stood at nearly 90% of its deposits. Emirates Islamic brings a large retail deposit base, which gives the combined bank more room to lend.

The deal pairs two different strengths. Dubai Islamic Bank is strong in corporate banking, while Emirates Islamic is strong in retail banking and deposits. The boards of both banks approved the deal on November 25. Dubai Islamic Bank chairman Fadhel Abdulbaqi Al Ali called it a defining moment for the bank and for Islamic finance. The deal values Emirates Islamic at about Dh31.1 billion (about $8.5 billion). Dubai Islamic Bank's shares have risen about 16% since details of the deal emerged.

For an Emirates Islamic customer in Dubai, nothing changes yet. No account, card or branch changes have been announced, and the deal cannot close until shareholders and regulators approve it. Bloomberg first reported talks between the two banks on November 23, and the boards met on November 25 to sign the agreement.

Guidance: what happens when your bank is bought

A customer of Emirates Islamic in Dubai woke up to news that her bank is being bought. Her account, her card and her app all still work the same way today. That is the first thing to know about a bank takeover: nothing changes for customers on the day the deal is announced.

A takeover moves through set steps. First the two banks sign a share purchase agreement, which is the contract that sets the price and the terms. Then shareholders vote on it, and the regulators check it. In this deal the Central Bank of the UAE and the Capital Markets Authority must both clear it. Only after those approvals does the deal close, and only then do the banks start combining their systems.

The brand usually stays for a while. Emirates Islamic has its own branches, its own app and its own customers, and combining two banks takes months of work behind the scenes. Customers get letters before anything changes about their accounts, and the new terms are set out in those letters.

What a customer should watch for is simple. Watch for a letter from the bank about account numbers, cards or the app. Watch for changes to profit rates on savings and financing. The bank's own announcements are the reliable source for what changes and when.

Guidance: how a financial advisor builds a halal portfolio

An advisor in Chicago sits down with a client who wants his savings invested the halal way. The first step is not picking stocks. It is agreeing on the screening rules the portfolio will follow, because different boards set different limits. Zoya follows the AAOIFI standards, which cap a company's interest-bearing debt and interest-bearing cash at 30% of its market value each, and impermissible income at 5% of revenue. Other index providers use one-third of total assets instead. The same company can pass one screen and fail another.

The second step is deciding what to do with Questionable holdings. These are companies in a grey area where scholars disagree, such as advertising platforms and supermarkets that sell alcohol. Zoya marks them Questionable and leaves the call to the investor. An advisor agrees this rule with the client before buying anything, so a later disagreement does not force a sale at the wrong time.

The third step is checking concentration. Screening reshapes the market. In Zoya's AAOIFI ratings on November 19, 2026, 54% of the S&P 500 by weight was Compliant, 20% Questionable and 26% Non-Compliant, with the Compliant names concentrated in technology. A portfolio built only from Compliant stocks ends up heavy in one sector, so the advisor spreads the money across sectors and checks the balance again after every market move.

The last step is monitoring. A company that passes today can fail tomorrow when a new filing lands, so the advisor re-checks each holding against the screen and flags any holding that stops passing. The figures in this piece are Zoya's own, from its November 2026 advisor guide.
