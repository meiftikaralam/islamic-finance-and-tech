Islamic Finance Daily Brief — Monday, October 19, 2026

Assalamu alaikum! Today's Islamic finance stories — and what to learn from them.

🇲🇾 1. SkyWorld sets up a MYR2 billion Sukuk Wakalah programme and issues its first MYR40 million

SkyWorld Capital, the financing arm of Malaysian property developer SkyWorld Group, has set up a MYR2 billion (about $489 million) Sukuk Wakalah programme and completed its first issuance on October 5: two series of MYR20 million each. Malaysian law firm Ong Eu Jin Partnership advised on the deal, which needed endorsement from the Securities Commission's Shariah Advisory Council under the Lodge and Launch framework. SkyWorld builds high-rise residential, commercial, and affordable housing in Kuala Lumpur and Penang, including its SkyAwani affordable series, with an 88.4% take-up rate across its projects.

What to learn: A programme, as covered in the Oct 16 edition (for more detail), is a pre-approved framework: once the regulator approves it, the company can sell multiple batches over time without starting the paperwork from scratch. What is new here is the Wakalah structure inside it.

Wakalah means agency. The investors appoint SkyWorld Capital as their agent. The agent takes the investors' money and puts it to work in the group's property development business, following an agreed set of rules. The returns come from the profits of that activity, not from interest. The agent earns a fee for the work.

The structure fits a builder. A property developer's business is buying land, building homes, and selling them — all real economic activity, which is exactly what Shariah-compliant financing requires. The sukuk investors fund that pipeline, and the completed homes are the asset behind their returns. For the homebuyer in Kuala Lumpur or Penang who buys a SkyAwani apartment, this is the financing that keeps the cranes moving. Today's Learn section explains Wakala in full.

Learn

Wakala: appointing someone to act on your behalf

What it is: Wakala means agency. You appoint a person or a company — the wakil — to do something for you. In Islamic finance, an investor gives money to an agent who invests it under an agreed set of rules. The agent earns a fixed fee for the work.

How it works: The agent must follow the mandate. The mandate says what the money can go into and what it must avoid. The profits belong to the investor, and the agent takes only the agreed fee. If the agent is careless or breaks the rules, the agent carries the loss. If the investment loses money through no fault of the agent, the investor carries it.

This differs from Mudaraba, covered in the Oct 13 edition (for more detail). In Mudaraba, the manager takes a share of the profits. In Wakala, the agent takes a fixed fee and the investor keeps all the profit above that fee. The fixed fee makes the cost clear upfront, which suits investors who want to know exactly what the management will cost.
