# SS 33: Waqf

## What this standard is about

Note: AAOIFI later issued SS 60 (Waqf), which replaces this standard with updated rules. The summary below covers SS 33 as originally published.

Waqf is one of the oldest charitable tools in Islam. A donor (the waqif) sets aside a property — a building, land, or even money — so that it can never be sold or given away again. The property itself is locked in place. Only its benefits (rent, produce, income) go to the people or causes the donor names.

The standard covers the full picture: what counts as waqf, its types, who can be a donor, what property qualifies, what conditions a donor may attach, and how the waqf must be managed and supervised. It also explains the role Islamic banks can play in growing waqf assets through safe, halal investment.

It does not cover other charitable structures, such as irsad (public property set aside for public use) or conventional financial trusts.

## Why it exists

Waqf assets are meant to last for generations, so disputes arise: can the donor take the property back? Can the manager sell it? Who watches the manager? Without clear rules, waqf money can be mismanaged or frozen in unproductive assets. This standard gives donors, managers, and Islamic banks one shared rulebook.

## The key rules, simply put

- Waqf is a binding commitment. Once property is declared waqf, the donor loses ownership of it immediately.
- The main types are charitable waqf (benefits go to charity), family waqf (benefits go to named family members, then to charity when none remain), joint waqf (shared between family and charity), and self-dedicated waqf (the donor keeps the benefits during his life; charity receives them after his death).
- The donor can be a person or a company, and must be legally able to give the property away.
- The property must be clearly identified, halal in nature, and fully owned by the donor.
- Waqf can be made in land, buildings, movable goods, money, and even shares or sukuk. With money waqf, the principal is preserved and only the income is spent.
- In principle waqf is permanent, but a temporary waqf for a fixed period is allowed.
- The donor may attach conditions — for example, naming the manager, or ordering that poor relatives are helped before others — as long as the conditions do not break Islamic rules. A bad condition is dropped; the waqf itself stays valid.
- The manager must maintain the property, keep accounts, lease it at fair rent, and spend income on the named beneficiaries. He may not lease it to himself or use it for personal gain.
- If the waqf property is ruined or becomes useless, it can be exchanged for a better one (istibdal) under strict conditions.
- The waqf has its own legal personality, separate from its manager.

## An everyday example

A woman owns an apartment building. She declares it waqf, with the rental income to pay school fees for her grandchildren, and after them, for poor students. She no longer owns the building and cannot sell it. A manager collects the rent, maintains the building, and distributes the income exactly as she instructed.

## Words to know

- **Waqf** — a property dedicated permanently so its benefits go to a named cause. The property cannot be sold or inherited.
- **Waqif** — the donor who creates the waqf.
- **Usufruct** — the right to use something and benefit from it, like living in a building or collecting its rent.
- **Istibdal** — exchanging a waqf property for another one when the original is ruined or no longer useful.
- **Riba (interest)** — any guaranteed extra charged on a loan. Not allowed in Islamic finance.
- **Halal** — permissible under Islamic rules.
- **Mudarabah** — a partnership where one side provides money and the other provides work; profits are shared.
- **Sukuk** — certificates giving ownership in a real asset, earning returns from what the asset produces.
